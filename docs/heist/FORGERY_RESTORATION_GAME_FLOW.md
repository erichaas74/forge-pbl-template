# Restore the collection: student forgery-restoration activity

The core restoration loop is now built as project **2.0.0**. See [implementation, capabilities and verification](RESTORATION_IMPLEMENTATION.md) for the delivered 16-painting studio, living ledger and final recovery. This design document also includes future extensions: only the coastal story environment is currently available; other commissions use the research desk. The [story portal ideas](STORY_PORTAL_FLOW_IDEAS.md) serve this restoration loop.

## New goal and student briefing

**Restore every assigned forged painting so its details and claims fit the assigned historical time and place. Support each repair with evidence and explain the finished reconstruction.**

> The collection has been altered. Some paintings contain objects from the wrong century. Others misrepresent a place, a voyage, a community, or the origins of a cargo. You are the museum's historical restoration team. Enter the stories, investigate the details, and repair the paintings. Keep what the evidence supports. Explain what you changed. When the collection is ready, use your knowledge in the final heist.

The student produces a visible before-and-after painting and a short restoration record. The product is labeled **Student historical reconstruction**. Preserve the original forged version so the student's changes remain inspectable.

## Core student loop

**Receive a restoration commission → inspect the painting → research through a story portal → change a detail → explain the repair → review the complete scene → exhibit the reconstruction.**

Research and editing can alternate. A student who encounters an unfamiliar instrument should be able to visit the workshop story, return to the same selected instrument, and continue the repair. Later students can work from their previously collected sources; repeating every narration is unnecessary.

The assigned target is explicit and stable: time, place, and event/context. Students cannot make an anachronistic object acceptable simply by changing the commission's target century. An incorrect date painted on a document or written in a caption can still be repaired to match that target. Reinterpreting a painting for a different period would be a separate teacher-assigned challenge.

## The restoration studio

Keep the painting large and central. Surround it with a few understandable tools:

- **Inspect:** choose a detail in the painting or from its accessible detail list.
- **Research this detail:** open a relevant story portal, interview, or reference note.
- **Change this part:** open the tools and candidate materials for that selected region.
- **Explain my repair:** attach a source and a short claim/evidence statement.
- **Compare before & after:** switch versions or use a comparison slider.
- **Submit reconstruction:** review the whole work after the assigned repairs are ready.

Show the commission at the top and a small restoration ledger alongside the canvas. Provide undo and saved drafts. Candidate names describe objects or claims without labeling an option “the correct answer.”

### Things students can actually change

| Tool | Student action | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Replace an object | Select a depicted object, inspect alternatives, then choose a replacement | Replace a reflecting sextant in a 1500 workshop with an instrument supported by the reference |
| Remove an intrusion | Lift an object out of the scene and reveal a prepared background patch | Remove established European horse herds from the assigned pre-contact shore |
| Repair an inscription | Edit a dated banner, document, caption, sponsor name, or crate label | Correct the treaty date or a voyage's sponsor |
| Correct a relationship | Move origin/destination labels or redraw a cargo/route arrow | Keep potatoes in a later cargo but correct their stated origin to the Americas |
| Repair a setting detail | Choose a source-supported landscape or built-environment patch | Correct a misplaced geographic feature where the depicted setting actually conflicts with the commission |
| Keep a supported detail | Mark a detail as retained and explain why | Preserve maize or a supported navigation instrument rather than replacing everything |

Place, community, and sponsorship errors are not appearance-matching exercises. If a Caribbean scene has an incorrect “Inca” caption, repair the caption using geographic evidence. Do not ask students to assign a community by changing skin color or choosing a stereotyped costume. Alter the depicted setting only when a specific visual claim is also wrong and the available references support a replacement.

## Worked student example: the shore painting

Commission: reconstruct the claimed Hispaniola setting before European contact.

1. Open the painting. A novice can reveal broad inspection regions; an advanced student identifies the region independently.
2. Select the depicted horse herd. Record the suspicion: “The animals may not fit this period.”
3. Choose **Visit the shore story** or consult an already collected exchange reference. Listen/read, ask the timing question, and distinguish the reconstruction from its underlying source.
4. Return to the selected herd. Choose **Remove this detail**. The horses visibly disappear and the prepared shore background becomes visible.
5. Retain the maize/canoe details if the reference and scene context support them. Filling every empty area is not a requirement.
6. Attach the exchange reference and complete: **“I changed ___ because ___. The reference says ___.”** For this case, the relevant contradiction is the claim of established European horse herds before contact.
7. Compare before and after. Submit the reconstruction with its target time/place and explanation.

An alternative replacement may be accepted when the commissioned context and sources support it. Do not require every student to make an identical decorative composition.

## Research portals become useful destinations

Put one visible **Story Portal** near the restoration studio. From a selected detail, its label becomes specific: **Research this animal**, **Visit the navigator's workshop**, or **Check the voyage record**. Give a short purpose before entry: “Find a reference that can establish when this tool was available.”

Inside, keep the proposed seat → story → question → object observation path, with free exploration. Replace the vague exit with **Return to my painting**. Restore the student's selected region and show the collected reference beside it. A chapter opened is access; the student's supported repair is the assessed action.

The portal does not select the repair for the student. Story imagery is a learning aid, not independent proof of the accuracy of that same reconstructed imagery. Narrator identity and source attribution remain visible.

## Support students without turning it into guessing

**First two paintings:** one major error each, broad inspection cues, a short source list, guided editing, and an explanation sentence stem.

**Middle collection:** students identify the questionable region, compare several plausible options, and justify a repair. Add a “keep this detail” decision so changing everything is not rewarded.

**Independent commissions:** combine two or three errors in newly authored artwork, include an already correct suspicious-looking detail, and ask for a whole-scene defense. These combined paintings are future content; the current sixteen mainly test one designated contradiction each.

Use hints in stages: point toward a reference; highlight the relevant part of that reference; then offer a worked comparison if needed. Let students revise. Do not reward rapid option cycling, penalize reading speed, or require fine drawing skill to demonstrate historical understanding.

A proposed replacement should remain visible as a draft even if the student needs more evidence. Feedback can say, “Which reference places this instrument in the assigned period?” rather than supplying the solution immediately. Structured choices can receive deterministic feedback; nuanced free-written explanations remain available for teacher review.

## What gets assessed and saved

Assess four things separately:

1. The assigned historical contradictions were repaired.
2. Supported details were preserved and the finished scene fits the commission.
3. Each repair has a relevant source supporting the new claim.
4. The student explains the change and what the evidence can establish.

Do not grade decorative style or precise pointer placement as historical mastery. Accept multiple authored, evidence-supported solutions. A repair is not verified merely because a student clicked every tool or opened every story.

Rename the running fraud-clue list **Restoration ledger** in the future experience. Each entry retains painting/region, original claim, student's suspicion, original and replacement parts, source, explanation, revision history, and review status. Suggested statuses: **Needs research → Draft repair → Ready for review → Verified reconstruction**. Record access and learning assessment separately.

The final artifact contains the original image, restored image, a before/after comparison, numbered repair annotations, sources, and the student's museum label. Keep the immutable original and student revision history available.

## Classroom structure and the final heist

Use rotating roles: **Inspector**, **Researcher**, **Restorer**, and **Curator**. Rotate after each painting so each student participates in research and explanation. Solo mode uses the same tools. At review, another team can ask, “What did you keep, what did you change, and what source supports that decision?”

Make restoration the learning phase across the preceding sessions. The full sixteen-painting collection with stories is a larger commitment than the earlier short capstone; time the first two commissions before setting a fixed lesson budget.

Students restore digital study copies during the learning phase. Those reconstructions and their ledger become the team's field guide for the final heist, which retrieves the threatened collection. This makes the earlier restoration work useful without requiring the student to have already physically recovered the objects.

The final heist remains at the end: use familiar source judgments and applied mathematics to operate the final vault and plan extraction, recover the collection, then present the reconstructed exhibition. Students use their earlier restoration ledger during this application. Avoid requiring sixteen repairs and the same sixteen unrelated recovery locks; retain mechanisms where they have a clear instructional or final-heist purpose.

## Practical first build

Start with the first shore forgery and the 1500 sextant forgery. Together they test removal, replacement, source matching, return from a portal, preserving correct details, and before/after review. Follow with a caption/date commission before authoring the entire collection.

Use layered artwork: an intact background, separately removable incorrect objects, prepared background patches, approved replacement assets, and editable labels/overlays. Flattened paintings need this art preparation before students can make convincing visual repairs. The current sprite-sheet painting presentation alone cannot perform arbitrary object edits.

Keep a reusable configuration for historical targets, editable regions, allowed tools, candidate assets, accepted repair/source combinations, and review prompts. Store student edits separately from published artwork. Asset production and this editing/assessment capability are new work; do not change the published mission's goal text to promise tools that are not yet available.

No application code, published package, artwork, or student save was changed for this brainstorm.
