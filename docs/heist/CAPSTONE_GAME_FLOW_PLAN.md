# The Cartographer’s Vault: capstone game-flow plan

Design draft · 2026-09-13 · `/projects/shadow-gallery`

This document plans the next experience. The game has not been changed to implement it. It contains student prompts and an author/teacher answer key; do not publish this complete document inside the student game.

**Revised primary goal:** [Restore every assigned forged painting](FORGERY_RESTORATION_GAME_FLOW.md) by changing incorrect image parts and claims to fit the assigned historical time and place, with evidence for each repair. Stories and portals support that research. Restoration becomes the learning phase; the final heist applies that learning at the end. The detailed recovery/lock sequence below is the earlier draft and should be adapted to this goal, not added as a second compulsory workload.

**Interaction-layer extension:** [Living scenes: enter the painting, meet people, hear their stories](LIVING_SCENES_INTERACTION_PLAN.md) makes immersive first-person-style encounters part of this plan. Students can enter reconstructed environments, sit for story time, ask follow-up questions, watch attributed event reconstructions and try small simulations. This replaces some text-only reference/NPC interactions and adds extended exploration during the unit; its timing and delivery sequence refine the stages below. The final heist remains at the end, drawing on those encounters and their underlying sources.

**Purpose:** students reconstruct historically supported scenes by inspecting forged paintings, researching their claims, visibly changing incorrect parts, preserving supported details, and explaining their choices. They assemble a restored collection and evidence ledger, then apply their historical reasoning and mathematics in the final heist and extraction.

**Working assumptions:** grades 6–8; two 50-minute sessions; teams of 3–4 sharing one device, with an equivalent solo mode. These are planning assumptions, not confirmed classroom constraints. The design also supports a saved pause between sessions. Younger students can receive visual timelines, fraction diagrams, and worked examples without changing the historical reasoning target.

Historical content and baseline solutions come from the current project package and [the existing clue audit](CLUE_AND_VAULT_AUDIT.md). Characters, museum records, security systems, and emergencies below are fictional game devices. Paintings are reconstructions. A detail fitting a historical claim does not establish that an object is an original artifact, and an incorrect label alone does not establish deceptive intent.

## 1. Mission and learning outcomes

**Student briefing:**

> The Cartographer’s collection has been altered. Your team must restore each assigned painting to its historical time and place. Enter the stories, inspect the details, replace or remove what does not belong, and repair misleading inscriptions. Keep what the evidence supports and explain every repair. When the reconstructed collection is ready, use your evidence to complete the final heist and recover it safely.

The objective tracker begins with three empty silhouettes. Recovered evidence gradually restores each silhouette. Opening a passage visibly contributes to the collection’s recovery.

| Learning outcome | Observable student action | Evidence retained |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Test a historical claim against time and place | Select a specific detail, a relevant reference, and the relationship between them | Claim/evidence link and any revision |
| Distinguish origin, destination, and later movement | Correct cargo labels and apply the same origin rule to another crop | Completed manifest and transfer response |
| Use chronology accurately | Reconstruct a voyage and keep arrival, departure, and treaty dates separate | Timeline and final record assignments |
| Apply arithmetic to a physical constraint | Calculate an angle, distance, or capacity and set the mechanism accordingly | Calculation, setting, load and attempts |
| Evaluate what evidence cannot establish | Reject an unsupported conclusion about authenticity, identity, or consent | Selected limitation and short explanation |
| Revise a plan after new information | Change a route, load, or classification with a stated reason | Before/after decision and triggering evidence |

**Completion:** recover all three record sets, finish all seven gallery evidence bundles, resolve required audit questions, complete the vault and extraction, and save an individual debrief. Students do not have to expose all 16 optional frauds or collect every bonus item.

## 2. Session structure and navigation

| Phase | Target time | Student experience |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Briefing, role choice, reconnaissance and plan | 10 min | Establish objectives, inspect the map, choose a wing order |
| First encounter | 8 min | Learn the game’s inspection and evidence controls using familiar content |
| Navigator’s workshop | 7 min | Resolve a collector’s instrument claim |
| Departure port | 7 min | Reconstruct the expedition record and question a clerk |
| Cargo market | 9 min | Correct a manifest and adapt to a damaged cart |
| Route hall | 9 min | Plot a route and select a measured crossing |
| Community archive | 8 min | Repair misplaced labels and test an unsupported inference |
| Treaty room | 8 min | Verify signatories and distinguish a claim from consent |
| Final vault | 12 min | Assemble the collection’s evidence and solve combined challenges |
| Extraction | 7 min | Choose and execute a feasible recovery route |
| Debrief and exhibit reveal | 10 min | Explain a correction, a revision, and a limitation |
| Save/resume and transitions | 5 min | Pause near the end of the first session |
| **Total** | **100 min** | Targets for playtesting, not automatic countdowns |

The first implementation keeps the current room order. A later route-planning release can offer two orders after the common first encounter:

- **Navigation wing first:** workshop → port → route hall → cargo → community archive → treaty → vault.
- **Collection wing first:** cargo → community archive → workshop → port → route hall → treaty → vault.

Both routes cover the same essential learning. Wing order lets a team choose where to start; later resource decisions create the stronger tactical consequences. Sources needed to solve a room remain available regardless of order. No answer depends on a record that the selected route has made inaccessible.

The route map shows inspected, unresolved, completed, and next locations. Completed galleries can be reviewed through saved exhibits; revisiting a room never grants a reward twice. Students may bookmark an optional fraud for a later visit before extraction.

## 3. Roles, decisions and feedback

| Responsibility | Tool/action | Example contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Historian | Compare claim and source cards | “Which detail conflicts with the date?” |
| Navigator | Operate map, compass and scale | “What calculation justifies this setting?” |
| Conservator | Correct labels and check the recovery load | “Which description can we defend, and what can we safely carry?” |
| Operations lead | Track objectives and select the next action | “What evidence or equipment does this route need?” |

Rotate responsibilities after two galleries. Everyone can inspect sources and use accessibility controls. On one device, role prompts organize discussion; they do not require separate accounts. Solo play exposes all four tools without requiring role changes. Do not describe this pilot as synchronized multiplayer.

Use three short actions for required reasoning: **select the claim → attach relevant evidence → choose the relationship** (supports, contradicts, or does not establish). Do not show only a room number or an answer letter. Require a short written explanation at two important decisions and during the debrief, rather than after every click.

**Feedback ladder:**

1. Neutral diagnostic: name the unresolved relationship or mismatched control.
2. Evidence pointer: highlight the relevant reference card or the scene’s time/place.
3. Reasoning scaffold: offer a timeline, origin/destination comparison, or arithmetic setup.
4. Worked recovery: model the solution, then ask a small related check before continuing.

Reading, asking for a hint, using accessibility support, and revising an academic answer do not reduce the learning score. Record assistance for teacher interpretation. Avoid permanent lockouts and repeated identical practice as punishment.

Optional suspense can use an **alert meter from 0–3** in a later release. Only an explicitly labelled risky operation, such as forcing an unchecked access panel, increases it. At 3, trigger one announced route complication, then provide a guaranteed alternate route. Keep alert separate from academic correctness and use no live countdown by default. The initial pilot should prove the evidence loop before adding this system.

## 4. Reconnaissance and mission planning

**Scene:** a preparation desk with three target-record silhouettes, a museum floor plan, a route preview and the first reference packet. Students drag or select objective cards onto the plan.

| Student question | Challenge/action | Answer criteria and feedback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “What must we recover, and what must we establish about it?” | Match each record set to the claims it must support | Coastal arrival: date and community context; expedition: departure, destination and sponsor; treaty: date and parties. The checklist remains visible. |
| “Which statements in the briefing are confirmed, and which are still claims?” | Mark supported instructions separately from untested catalogue descriptions | The curator’s mission establishes the authorized objective. The disputed labels still require checking; official-looking formatting is not corroboration. |
| “Which wing should we investigate first, and why?” | Choose a wing order and an operational reason | Either order is acceptable. Accept a reason tied to the skills or evidence the team wants to use first. Do not mark a route wrong for being different. |
| “What will we do when two records disagree?” | Select a team response | Compare each claim with a relevant reference and preserve uncertainty. Majority vote, visual attractiveness and confidence alone do not resolve the discrepancy. |

**Payoff:** the route illuminates, role tools become available, and the curator gives a concise radio acknowledgement. The plan is saved and may be revised at a junction.

## 5. First encounter: a date, a place and an impossible detail

**Mission purpose:** establish the Coastal Arrival Folio’s setting. Keep the current three paintings: pre-contact horse herds, maize with a canoe, and an Inca-labelled Caribbean settlement.

**Student action:** inspect one claim in all three reconstructions, attach a relevant source to the selected compatible scene, and resolve at least one selected contradiction. This is a proposed capstone-mode gate; the current game permits a direct authentic choice without examining the other two scenes. Optional full fraud repair still remains a separate branch.

| ID | Exact student prompt | Teacher/author answer criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S1 | “The frame says Hispaniola in 1492. Which two things must a detail fit?” | Both the claimed place and time. The date alone does not settle the community or crop claim. |
| S2 | “The detail says these European horse herds were already established before arrival. What makes that claim fail?” | The pre-contact timing conflicts with the reference describing introduction from the Old World after contact. “Horses look unusual” is insufficient. |
| S3 | “Which reference would test the label ‘Inca island settlement’?” | Community/place evidence: Taíno Caribbean context and Inca Andes context. Clothing or facial appearance is not evidence of community identity. |
| S4 | “Which scene’s inspected details are compatible with this setting? Attach the strongest available reference.” | Passage II, maize and a dugout canoe. Relevant Caribbean/exchange evidence supports the claimed setting. This does not prove an original painting’s authenticity. |
| S5 | “The passage gear needs 3/8 of one clockwise turn from north. What angle should you calculate and set?” | `360 × 3/8 = 135°`; calculation and physical setting both 135. |
| S6 · optional | “Would a painting showing a horse anywhere in the Americas at any later date automatically be false?” | No. The specific place, introduction history and timing matter. Students must not turn this example into ‘all horses in America are impossible.’ |

**Character exchange:** the collector says, “Horses are common here today, so this scene must fit.” Students choose a follow-up. “Does the label claim before or after introduction?” prompts the collector to expose the timing error. “Do you like horses?” supplies no relevant evidence; the player can ask again.

**Recovery:** after a wrong classification, highlight the phrase “before European arrival” without immediately naming the category. After a math mismatch, show the full circle divided into eighths and let the student move three sections. Keep the existing crate recovery for the horse branch, but avoid making it a second required origin drill on the main path; the cargo room owns the full manifest challenge.

**Earned evidence E1:** verified coastal setting, selected supporting reference, and the corrected contradiction. The folio silhouette gains a coastline. This bundle is required in the vault’s record assembly.

## 6. Navigator’s workshop: challenge the collector

**Mission purpose:** establish whether a navigation record belongs to the claimed period. The fictional collector is confident; the student must assess the claim.

**Challenge:** sort four labelled instruments into the 1500 workshop or a later-instrument cabinet. Reveal the drawer contents and a short mechanical animation after a successful operation. Correct a catalogue card as part of the action.

| ID | Exact student prompt | Teacher/author answer criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| W1 | “The catalogue says 1500. Which instruments can belong in this workshop?” | Magnetic compass and mariner’s astrolabe. |
| W2 | “Which two labels conflict with that date, and what reference establishes the conflict?” | Reflecting sextant and Harrison marine timekeeper; their eighteenth-century development is later than 1500. |
| W3 | “The collector says the timekeeper looks old. What question would test its date?” | Ask about its development/date or documented history. Appearance and polish do not establish whether it existed in 1500. |
| W4 | “Which correction preserves the instrument record accurately?” | Identify the timekeeper as belonging to a later period; remove it from the 1500 reconstruction. Do not simply erase its label or mislabel it as an astrolabe. |
| W5 | “Does one correct compass prove the entire painting is historically compatible?” | No. Other labelled details could still contradict the setting; every claim being relied on needs checking. |

**Evidence-choice distractors:** an attractive frame, the collector’s confidence, a reference about instrument development, and an unrelated voyage date. The development reference is relevant; explain why the unrelated date does not settle the instrument question.

**Recovery:** place the scene date and the reference’s century on a visible timeline, then let the student revise the drawer placement. The model should not claim that a timekeeper stops existing after its invention period.

**Earned evidence E2:** a checked instrument inventory and the repaired catalogue entry. In the vault, it lets students challenge a technology claim inside the Coastal Arrival Folio. Remove the repeated mandatory west-bearing lock from this room in the proposed capstone mode; direction work is concentrated in the route hall and final vault.

## 7. Departure port: reconstruct a disputed expedition

**Mission purpose:** establish the Expedition Atlas’s departure, sponsor and arrival sequence.

**Challenge:** assemble a torn register from movable event strips and question a shipping clerk. One incorrect label is a transcription error; students can correct the claim without accusing the clerk of intentional fraud.

| ID | Exact student prompt | Teacher/author answer criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P1 | “Place these events from earliest to latest.” | Caribbean arrival 1492 → Treaty of Tordesillas 1494 → da Gama departs Lisbon 1497 → da Gama reaches India 1498. |
| P2 | “Two slips claim to date da Gama’s first departure for India: 1497 and 1607. Which does the expedition reference support?” | 1497. Attach `port-voyage`; explain that the claims refer to the same departure event. |
| P3 | “The clerk’s manifest calls this a French-sponsored voyage. Which field needs correction?” | Sponsor/national attribution → Portugal. This is separate from the departure-date field. |
| P4 | “The arrival was in 1498. Why can’t that date simply replace the departure date?” | Arrival and departure are different events; the voyage began in 1497. |
| P5 | “What does the mismatch establish about the clerk?” | It establishes an incorrect record. The evidence presented does not by itself establish deliberate deception. |

**NPC questions:** “What does this date label describe?”, “Which reference did you copy?”, and “Did you mean departure or arrival?” reveal different parts of the clerk’s explanation. Students may inspect the complete transcript as text; audio is optional.

**Recovery:** sort the slips first into departure, arrival and other-event trays; then arrange the dates. Preserve the student’s original sequence beside the revision.

**Earned evidence E3:** a corrected departure register. Its sponsor/date fields later match the atlas, and it clears an optional manifest checkpoint without repeating the entire quiz.

## 8. Cargo market: build a feasible recovery load

**Mission purpose:** repair origin labels and prepare equipment for extraction.

**Challenge A:** students place four goods into their origin crates and correct a reversed potato shipping label. A map arrow visualizes movement from origin to destination.

| ID | Exact student prompt | Teacher/author answer criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C1 | “Sort the goods by where they originated before the exchange represented here.” | Horse and wheat → Old World; maize and potato → Americas. |
| C2 | “The potato label says ‘European origin, introduced to the Americas.’ Which parts of that label need changing?” | The origin and direction are reversed. The corrected label identifies an American-origin crop moving to the Old World. |
| C3 | “A crop is grown in many places today. Does that establish where it originated?” | No. Present distribution and historical origin are different claims. |

**Challenge B:** the empty recovery cart is damaged before loading. Its initial 240 kg carrying capacity is reduced by 25%. The stated capacity concerns contents; the cart itself is excluded. Required contents are one 62 kg operative and the 78 kg collection case. The rest of the team uses a separate walkway and is not included in this cart’s load.

| ID | Exact student prompt | Teacher/author answer criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C4 | “What is the damaged cart’s new carrying limit? Show the retained fraction or percentage.” | 75% remains; `240 × 0.75 = 180 kg`. The 60 kg loss is not the remaining capacity. |
| C5 | “How much capacity remains after the operative and collection case are loaded?” | `62 + 78 = 140`; `180 − 140 = 40 kg`. |
| C6 | “Choose the 25 kg repair tools, the 40 kg spare equipment, or neither. Which loads are possible?” | Tools: 165 kg; spares: 180 kg; neither: 140 kg. Both optional loads together: 205 kg, infeasible. |
| C7 | “Which feasible load supports your intended extraction plan, and what are you giving up?” | Accept any feasible load with an accurate trade-off: tools can repair the lift, spares support the guided rail, neither uses the staffed corridor. All routes recover the complete collection. |

**Game consequence:** carry the selected equipment forward. The route preview explains its use before the choice. The player can unload or exchange optional equipment before the extraction commitment; no unseen penalty turns a previously reasonable choice into failure.

**Recovery:** if overloaded, show a mass breakdown and the excess. Let students remove equipment. If 60 is entered as capacity, ask, “Is that the amount lost or the amount left?”

**Earned evidence E4:** corrected origin manifest and saved load plan. The manifest supports the folio; the equipment changes extraction options. First short written response: one or two sentences justifying the load.

## 9. Route hall: navigate a changed crossing

**Mission purpose:** reconstruct the Expedition Atlas route and apply map mathematics to the museum’s recovery passage.

Keep the historical voyage map and the fictional museum blueprint visually distinct, with clear titles and units. Distances on the museum blueprint are not claims about a transoceanic voyage.

| ID | Exact student prompt | Teacher/author answer criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R1 | “Choose the main outward waypoints of da Gama’s voyage, in order.” | Lisbon → southern Africa → India. The Caribbean is a distractor belonging to a different voyage context. |
| R2 | “Which date and sponsor let you connect this map with the port register?” | Portuguese expedition; Lisbon departure 1497 and India arrival 1498 describe its sequence. |
| R3 | “The museum passage is 7.5 cm on a plan at 1 cm : 4 m. How far must the route extend?” | `7.5 × 4 = 30 m`. Enter 30 and set the extension to 30 m. |
| R4 | “The first crossing has closed. The alternate measures 5.5 cm at the same scale. Will a 24 m walkway reach?” | `5.5 × 4 = 22 m`; yes, leaving 2 m of reach. Set the alternate extension to 22 m. |
| R5 | “This passage faces west. What clockwise bearing from north should you set?” | 270°. East is 90°; the reference direction and clockwise convention matter. |
| R6 · optional | “Would the same 5.5 cm measurement represent the same distance on a different scale?” | Not necessarily. Distance depends on both the measurement and the scale. |

**Choice:** compare a 30 m original crossing needing repair with the 22 m alternate crossing available now. Students choose a currently feasible route. Taking the alternate remains possible without any optional cargo equipment; equipment benefits later extraction rather than blocking this core lesson.

**Recovery:** show unit labels beside the calculation; provide a scale strip of repeated 4 m segments. For the bearing, offer north/east/south/west markers before supplying a worked answer.

**Earned evidence E5:** the expedition route and the museum crossing calculation. The atlas gains its illuminated sea lane. The final vault reuses the directional convention with a new fraction of a turn.

## 10. Community archive: repair labels and state the limits

**Mission purpose:** restore the Coastal Arrival Folio’s community context and expose misleading catalogue language.

**Challenge:** two display labels have been exchanged. Students move each to its documented region and assemble a corrected label from supported phrases. A dramatic image alone does not supply a community identification.

| ID | Exact student prompt | Teacher/author answer criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | “Place the Inca and Taíno community labels in the regions supported by the references.” | Inca → Andes; Taíno → Caribbean islands. |
| A2 | “Which evidence tests this regional identification?” | The documented place/community relationship. Reject guesses based on clothing, skin colour or facial appearance. |
| A3 | “The catalogue calls an Andean mountain settlement a Taíno Caribbean village. What is the smallest accurate correction?” | Correct the community/region identification to the supported Andean Inca context; do not invent an exact settlement name or biography. |
| A4 | “The label says Caribbean history began when Europeans arrived in 1492. Which part is unsupported?” | European arrival does not mark the beginning of Caribbean history; communities already lived there. |
| A5 | “Can these references tell us the name of the particular person shown?” | No. They establish regional context, not that individual’s identity. ‘Not enough evidence’ is the correct outcome for this question. |

**Corrected label example:** “This reconstruction represents a Caribbean setting associated with Taíno communities. The 1492 date refers to European arrival, not the beginning of the community’s history.” Accept equivalent supported wording; do not require this exact sentence.

**Recovery:** display “What the reference establishes” and “What it does not establish” trays. Students move the specific-person claim into the second tray and revise their label.

**Earned evidence E6:** community provenance note and supported label. Second short written response: explain one correction or one limit. This label appears in the restored exhibit and is required to finish the folio’s vault record.

## 11. Treaty room: verify an agreement without overstating it

**Mission purpose:** establish the Treaty Register’s date and parties, then distinguish a European territorial claim from Indigenous consent.

**Challenge:** reconstruct two seals, a date plaque and an interpretation label. A forged 1607 plaque and a France seal are available as distractors.

| ID | Exact student prompt | Teacher/author answer criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| T1 | “Which year belongs on the Treaty of Tordesillas register?” | 1494. Enter the year and set the four date cylinders. |
| T2 | “Which two crowns belong on this agreement?” | Spain and Portugal. |
| T3 | “One label names Spain and France. What reference would resolve the disagreement?” | The treaty reference identifying the parties; the voyage sponsor alone is not sufficient evidence for this different agreement. |
| T4 | “Does an agreement between those crowns prove that Indigenous peoples consented to their claims?” | No. The agreement documents the crowns’ claims; the supplied evidence does not establish Indigenous consent. |
| T5 | “Select the interpretation this document can support.” | ‘Spain and Portugal made an agreement about their claims’ is supported. ‘All peoples living in the claimed lands agreed’ is not established. |

**NPC exchange:** an archivist says, “Both seals are present, so everyone must have agreed.” The player presents the signatory evidence and selects the limitation. The archivist revises the label; the game rewards the correction, not an accusation about motive.

**Recovery:** highlight who signed, then ask whether the missing group is represented by those signatures. Keep the distinction between “not established by this evidence” and a claim about every individual’s views.

**Earned evidence E7:** authenticated treaty date/parties and a qualified interpretation label. The register silhouette gains its seals. The original generic evidence-category sorting board can become an optional rehearsal; final required work connects evidence to specific claims instead.

## 13. The final vault: use the whole case

The vault is at the end of the historical investigation. Students can consult their evidence board. The challenge is selecting and applying the right evidence. No new historical facts are required here.

**Readiness check:** E1–E7 complete; selected facts and corrections are preserved; unresolved required reasoning steps link back to their challenge. Optional frauds are clearly labelled optional. Never announce a final surprise requirement the team had no chance to collect.

**Vault opening scene:** three record cases sit inside concentric rings. Students restore their labels on the outer table, then operate four inner mechanisms. Each completed layer reveals a visible part of the collection.

### Outer table: restore three record sets

| Case | Exact student question | Required evidence and accepted assembly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coastal Arrival Folio | “Which arrival date, community context and crop claim belong together? Which instrument claim must be excluded?” | E1/E2/E4/E6: 1492 Caribbean arrival; supported Taíno Caribbean context; maize of American origin; exclude a Harrison timekeeper from a 1492 scene. Include the arrival/history distinction. |
| Expedition Atlas | “Which departure, sponsor and route form one supported expedition?” | E3/E5: Lisbon 1497; Portugal; around southern Africa; India 1498. Do not substitute Columbus’s Caribbean arrival date. |
| Treaty Register | “Which date and seals belong together, and what does the agreement fail to establish?” | E7: 1494; Spain and Portugal; the crowns’ agreement does not establish Indigenous consent. |

**Evidence challenge:** require one appropriate reference link per record relationship, not just a correct year. Use a configured accepted-evidence/relationship check. Save free-text explanations for review; never claim that a word count or keyword match demonstrates sound reasoning.

**Recovery:** illuminate only the unresolved relationship: “This is an arrival date attached to a departure field,” or “This reference names a voyage sponsor; this field asks about treaty parties.” Let students compare two saved cards side by side.

### Inner mechanisms: four final operations

| Order | Student-facing challenge | Author solution | Why earlier play matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 · Date cylinders | “Set the year supported by the Coastal Arrival Folio. The other recovered records contain different dates.” | 1492 in calculation and cylinders | Students distinguish three historical contexts using their collected records. |
| 2 · Compass rings | “The new vault drive needs 5/8 of one clockwise turn from north. Calculate and align it.” | `360 × 5/8 = 225°`; set 225° | Applies the same fraction-of-a-turn rule to a new value. The proposed capstone changes the current vault’s 270° target and must use a new package version. |
| 3 · Manifest lock | “An added crate is labelled cacao and another sugarcane. Place them by origin using the manifest’s reference, then confirm horse, wheat, maize and potato.” | Cacao, maize, potato → Americas; sugarcane, horse, wheat → Old World | The existing origin reference already includes these crops. This checks transfer without teaching a new historical rule at the vault. |
| 4 · Treaty lever | “Portugal’s seal is fixed. Select the other signatory and attach the register’s qualified interpretation.” | Spain; agreement between crowns does not establish Indigenous consent | Requires both a factual match and the limitation previously reasoned through. |

Use visible mechanisms with tactile response: rotating rings, sliding labels, moving crates and aligning seals. Audio has captions and mute controls; reduced motion removes unnecessary movement. Numeric success requires both correct reasoning input and the physical setting, as in the current runtime.

**Before opening:** “Which one of your corrections prevents the collection from telling a misleading story?” Students choose one log entry and state its effect aloud or in a brief saved response. This is a discussion/assessment opportunity; do not invent a machine correctness judgment for unrestricted prose.

**Completion animation:** the three silhouettes become restored records. A curator confirms the collection is ready to move. Extraction is now available; it is a separate final decision.

## 14. Extraction: finish with a defensible choice

The collection case remains 78 kg and the operative remains 62 kg. No hidden mass is added after students commit to a load. The cart’s damaged carrying limit remains 180 kg. The same load is checked again against the selected route.

| Route | Requirements shown before commitment | Feasible load and outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conservation lift | 25 kg repair tools; total payload at most 170 kg after the fictional lift repair | 165 kg with tools fits both lift and cart. Repair and take the direct descent. |
| Guided rail | 40 kg spare equipment; total payload at most 180 kg | 180 kg fits exactly. Install the guides and use the rail. |
| Staffed corridor | No optional equipment; cart total at most 180 kg | Unload optional equipment if desired; 140 kg fits. Present a checked record at the staff checkpoint. This route is always available. |

These are fictional engineering constraints for the game. Route requirements and consequences are visible before selection. All three routes can recover all records; route choice is not a disguised single-correct-answer quiz.

| ID | Exact student prompt | Answer criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| X1 | “Which available exit can carry your current load? Compare both the cart and route limits.” | A choice satisfying every applicable limit and required equipment. Checking only one limit is incomplete. |
| X2 | “Your teammate wants to take both optional equipment sets. What happens to the load?” | 205 kg; exceeds the cart’s 180 kg capacity. Revise by leaving equipment, never a required record. |
| X3 | “What evidence or equipment makes your chosen route possible?” | Identify the relevant load/repair constraint or the checked record for the staff checkpoint. |
| X4 | “If the selected exit becomes unavailable, how can you preserve the collection?” | Replan to another currently feasible exit, including the always-available staffed corridor. Recheck the load after any equipment change. |

The optional alert complication can disable one nonessential exit once, before commitment. It cannot disable the staffed corridor or destroy earned evidence. The default pilot uses a scripted cart complication only, keeping the number of moving parts manageable.

## 15. Debrief and the restored exhibit

Display the three recovered records with the team’s supported labels. Replay three meaningful decisions: a contradiction they resolved, a plan they revised, and a useful evidence link. Avoid a long list of clicks.

Every student answers these prompts individually, orally or in writing according to classroom needs:

1. “Choose one incorrect claim. What exact detail was wrong, what reference did you use, and what correction follows?”
2. “Describe one decision you revised. What new evidence or constraint changed your reasoning?”
3. “Name one thing your evidence supported and one thing it could not establish.”
4. Optional team reflection: “What would you change about your route or division of responsibilities on another attempt?”

The teacher can assess three dimensions, each 0–3: **evidence and historical reasoning**, **mathematical application**, and **revision and explanation**. A 3 means an accurate relationship with relevant evidence or calculation and a clear explanation; a 2 has the correct conclusion with incomplete support; a 1 contains partial reasoning or an unresolved misconception; a 0 contains no usable evidence yet. Use dimension-specific examples from the question tables to anchor judgments.

Keep team mission completion and individual understanding visible separately. Do not infer an individual’s mastery from another player’s correct click. Do not use fastest completion, fewest hints or fewest revisions as the academic score. Optional recognition can celebrate an evidence-backed correction, a feasible alternate plan, or a clearly stated limitation.

## 16. Question presentation and difficulty

The tables are the question bank, not a requirement to put every prompt on a separate screen. Bundle related questions into the action they guide. For example, cargo-origin questions become one manifest workspace, and load questions become one interactive balance with a route preview. Reveal consequences beside the controls that caused them.

| Support level | What changes | What stays the same |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Supported | Read-aloud text, reduced text density, highlighted reference, century timeline, fraction wheel, labelled units | The student still selects evidence, makes the relationship and operates the mechanism |
| Standard | Sources available in notebook, neutral feedback, optional hint ladder | Full core mission and evidence requirements |
| Extension | Extra unsupported claim, less redundant evidence, an additional feasible route to compare | No requirement for previously untaught historical facts |

Provide keyboard/select-and-place alternatives, visible focus, readable contrast, captions, unit labels and a calculator if classroom policy permits. Grade supports according to the learning objective; calculator use need not remove the requirement to choose the correct operation.

Use one short reminder when a student gets stuck, then return them to the problem. Avoid inserting a long lecture between every room. Make the first encounter the main control tutorial; subsequent rooms can display a one-line reminder for a new interface.

## 17. Scope and implementation order

The current gallery already supports inspection, four fraud categories, optional recovery, ordered academic locks, source notes, a running audit, local saves, replay and exports. The following stages are proposed work. They are not descriptions of delivered behavior.

| Stage | Deliverable | What can be reused | Exit criterion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 · Evidence-driven room pilot | Briefing objective, first-room claim/evidence link, contextual feedback, visible earned record, revised room pacing | Existing inspection, source notes, locks, log and dossier | A student can explain why a detail conflicts with the setting, and the saved evidence visibly contributes to a target record |
| 2 · Complete the linear capstone | Room questions/challenges above, structured label corrections, three target records, outer vault assembly, new final values and crops | Current linear route and existing lock evaluators | All seven evidence bundles are reachable; the final vault uses their content; old practice still works under its original version |
| 3 · Choices with consequences | Equipment inventory, persistent load plan, extraction route preview, feasible alternate exits, review of prior rooms | Cargo mathematics, snapshots and audit presentation | Every equipment choice has a visible consequence and every reachable state has a valid recovery route |
| 4 · Route and character variation | Configurable wing order, scripted NPC exchanges, optional alert complication, role prompts and exhibit debrief | Existing shared LMS NPC/rule capabilities where adapters are appropriate | Both orders complete the same core learning, replay/restoration are deterministic, and no event grants duplicate rewards |
| 5 · Classroom pilot and revision | Observe two or more teams and solo play, review individual explanations, tune pacing and text | Test fixtures, exports and teacher observation | Students understand the objective, use evidence without constant adult prompting, and reach a meaningful debrief within the class allocation |

**First slice to build:** briefing → first encounter → earned Coastal Folio evidence → a miniature final evidence check. Prove the complete learning loop in one room before expanding every room. This is a development slice, not the planned placement of the full final heist in the classroom sequence.

## 18. Acceptance and classroom checks

- Every required question has its supporting reference available before the student must answer. Both wing orders satisfy this condition.
- Required and optional fraud work are visibly distinguished. Missing optional frauds never block the vault.
- Every final record cites earned evidence, and every required evidence bundle is used in at least one final relationship.
- Correct scene recognition alone is insufficient for capstone completion; students must establish the required claim/evidence relationships.
- A false historical claim, a mismatched calculation and a mismatched physical setting receive distinct useful feedback.
- The new 225° vault target and added crop cards are checked against the new version; old saves retain their original rules.
- All accepted loads and route combinations are validated, including 140, 165, 180 and 205 kg cases and the lift’s separate 170 kg limit.
- Equipment exchange, revisiting, duplicate requests and reloads cannot duplicate rewards or lose a record.
- Every reachable mission state retains a feasible extraction path. Alert can never destroy the learning record or create a dead end.
- Free-text reasoning remains visible for review; automated checks evaluate only defined structured relationships and numeric constraints.
- Keyboard, reduced-motion, captions, phone-sized layout, local save/resume, exports and replay remain functional.
- Test the reusable additions with a second, substantially different content fixture. A renamed copy alone does not establish broad reuse.
- During playtesting, record time by challenge, where students ask what to do, whether they can explain a clue, and whether role prompts broaden participation. Treat the 100-minute schedule as a hypothesis to adjust from observation.

## 19. Design decisions to confirm before full production

The plan proceeds with the defaults below; these are implementation checkpoints, not blockers to reviewing this draft.

| Decision | Proposed default | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Age and prior teaching | Grades 6–8; listed history and math already taught | Controls reading density, hints and the transfer questions |
| Class allocation | Two 50-minute sessions with saved progress | Controls how many optional branches can be meaningfully explored |
| Team setup | One device per team; individual debrief; solo supported | Keeps the first release within local-practice capabilities |
| Required fraud reasoning | Inspect all three first-room claims and resolve one contradiction; later optional frauds available | Ensures comparison while avoiding sixteen compulsory detours |
| Assessment | Teacher reviews a small set of reasoning responses; no speed grade | Keeps mission performance distinct from individual understanding |
| Suspense | Scripted cart complication first; optional alert later | Lets us validate the learning and recovery flow before adding more state |
| Return visits | Review evidence anytime; complete bookmarked optional work before extraction | Makes the running list useful without duplicate rewards |
| Full final vault | New combinations of already-taught facts and operations, after the gallery investigation | Preserves the intended end-of-unit capstone |
