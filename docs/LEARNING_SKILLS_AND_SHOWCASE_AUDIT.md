# Learning skills and showcase audit

September 2026. Pre-rebuild audit and corrective design. See [Student first steps: rebuild and review](STUDENT_FIRST_STEPS_REBUILD.md) for the implemented changes and remaining work.

## Finding

The showcases need to demonstrate students learning through a creative activity. Several openings currently demonstrate a theme, a click, and a supplied outcome. The core intellectual action is missing from the opening even where it exists later in the project.

The Trading practice is a confirmed instructional omission: the student chooses two items, while the application performs every calculation and explains the outcome. The final Trading presentation also generates its prominent equations from game records. Those are useful representations of the simulation, but they do not show how a learner arrived at an answer.

The previous audit overemphasized layout. Its guiding rule must be strengthened: **the central task is the learning operation itself—calculating, observing, comparing, interpreting, constructing an argument, or revising. Extra directions can be optional; the work and the evidence needed to do it cannot be hidden in Help.**

## Scope and method

Traced all seven catalog learning goals through their opening configuration, shared opening renderer, relevant main activity/validation code, and completed sample data/presentation. Used the current working tree; older audit claims were checked against current code rather than treated as present behavior. This evaluates the project's stated learning goals, not formal standards alignment or measured teaching effectiveness.

Fresh browser checks reproduced Trading's practice through the result, inspected its final Math slide, and inspected Journey's crisis replay and the disclosure path to its evidence/revision. The preceding simplicity audit inspected representative workspaces across all seven projects. Other findings here are source-based, with entry points below. No official trade, learner assessment, or publication was submitted. Browser practice selections were local demonstration interactions.

For each project the audit asks:

1. What skill is promised?
2. What must the student actually produce?
3. What feedback helps them improve it?
4. What is retained as student work, rather than generated by the system?
5. Can a viewer see the problem, thinking, and improvement in the showcase?

Watching a demonstration can introduce a concept. Clicking an option can also be a meaningful response. The failure occurs when the advertised skill requires mathematical work, evidence interpretation, or composition and neither the interaction nor its feedback elicits that work.

## Trading: confirmed failure and precise repair

### Current opening

The configured challenge has 30 coins, two cargo spaces, and three single-unit goods:

| Good | Purchase cost | Actual sale | Per-item result |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Rope | 8 | 15 | +7 |
| Cloth | 15 | 12 | −3 |
| Provisions | 10 | 14 | +4 |

The cloth description also advertises a predicted sale of 24. That forecast is a promising teaching opportunity, but the student never records a forecast or calculates its difference from the actual result.

Browser reproduction: select rope, then cloth, then **Head to market**. The UI immediately supplies cost 23, sales 27, profit 4, cash 34, and the equation `30 − 23 + 27 = 34`. There is no math input, model-building action, explanation, or revision before continuation.

Source confirms that `cargoTotals()` calculates all values; `depart()` checks the item count/budget and immediately reveals `cargoOutcome()`. `ProjectTeaserResult.observations` stores configured result strings. It contains no student numeric answer, calculation method, attempt, or feedback history.

Additional problems:

- Every allowed pair is affordable: totals are 23, 18, and 25. Budget feasibility does not distinguish the available pairs. Choosing two still involves opportunity cost, but the interface never asks students to compare it.
- Every two-item bundle produces a positive total result: +4, +11, or +1. Cloth's individual loss can disappear inside the combined gain.
- The rope/cloth reveal concatenates the cloth sentence “this practice trade ends with a loss” with a bundle profit of 4. The narration needs to distinguish **cloth lost 3** from **the combined trade gained 4**.
- All quantities are one and all prices are whole coins. This can be an intentional warm-up, but it does not yet demonstrate the catalog's decimal-money or multiplicative-reasoning goals.

### What already exists later

The current main simulation requires student-entered transaction totals, checks them in `commitTrade()`, requires correct sales/profit forecasts before departure, and rejects an incorrect trail math answer. These improvements are real; the statement “Trading never asks students to do math” would be inaccurate.

The remaining instructional weaknesses are:

- The opening uses a separate, calculation-free path rather than demonstrating the main project's mathematics.
- The main transaction form supplies the operation and checks the final number. Feedback such as “check the multiplication and discount” does not reveal the student's method or diagnose which step failed.
- Failed attempts are rejected without adding an attempt record in the inspected reducer path. The Math score uses accepted forecast/event records; transaction totals are not included in that calculation. A corrected completion can therefore earn full Math points without showing first-attempt accuracy or support used.
- Final report readiness checks response length, evidence count, and a nonempty calculation. It is a completeness check, not a check of the argument's mathematical validity.
- The main Math showcase slide displays system-derived forecast, actual-profit, and cash equations. Student-authored calculation text appears later with reflection evidence. The sample is generated using correct answers from the engine, appropriately as fictional data; it does not demonstrate a learner's attempted method and correction.

### Replace the practice with a short visible learning sequence

Keep the wagon and goods. Make a small editable receipt beside the selected cargo the central work surface. Present one operation at a time rather than a long worksheet.

| Step | Student does | Visible representation and feedback |
|---|---|---|
| Buy | Chooses goods and works out their total cost | Prices stay attached to goods. Student groups coins or enters `8 + 15 = [ ]`. Do not reveal the target total in another status badge first. |
| Budget | Works out how much remains | Starting purse, purchase receipt, and `[ ] coins left`. A bar/coin model can illustrate spending after the attempt. |
| Sell | Inspects the new offers and calculates total sales | Show the actual offers of 15 and 12. Ask for the combined receipt before revealing its answer. |
| Profit | Compares sales with the original cost | Student completes the difference or uses the coin model. Distinguish sales 27, profit 4, and final cash 34. Show cloth's loss and rope's gain separately. |
| Explain/change | Identifies what changed from the forecast and compares another load | A short recorded explanation or selection of evidence can support the comparison. Retain the original attempt and revised reasoning. |

For an initial error, keep the attempt visible and reveal a relevant model or hint. For example, if a student identifies 27 as profit, contrast the sales receipt with the original 23-coin cost. Do not jump immediately to a finished answer. Supported completion is valid; record support honestly. Open-ended strategy has multiple defensible answers and should not be forced into a single “correct” cargo choice.

Follow this warm-up with a visible decimal unit-price × quantity trade in the main project/showcase. That is where the advertised Grade 5 mathematics becomes explicit. Reuse the existing transaction/forecast logic through an appropriate practice adapter; do not maintain a separate arithmetic engine.

## Audit by project

### Science — The Unlabeled Shelf

**Promised:** properties of matter, evidence analysis, scientific argument.

**Currently enacted:** the opening runs a predetermined color comparison when the student presses the test button. The code records the configured blue/pink results, and the characters supply the inference that similar appearance does not establish identity. The student gives no prediction, comparison, or inference in that opening. Main stations are stronger: students manipulate instruments and must describe observations to file records. The final case asks for identification, evidence, reasoning, counterevidence, and a restoration recommendation. The sample includes controlled-test matrices and hypothesis revisions.

**Gap:** pressing Test demonstrates operation of a simulation. It does not demonstrate the observation-to-inference skill. The final case's field/evidence presence checks cannot establish that the explanation follows from the results.

**What to showcase centrally:** two similar samples → student's initial prediction → same test on both → student marks the observed difference → chooses which result weakens the original claim → revises the identification. Keep “what I saw” and “what I think it means” distinct. Make a controlled comparison visible rather than letting the animation or an NPC supply the conclusion.

**Proof in the final showcase:** original claim, measured comparison, cited interpretation key, revised claim, and a remaining uncertainty. Let the viewer open the actual relevant test record from the claim. The existing sample's matrices/revisions are useful foundations.

### Museum — Objects That Changed Us

**Promised:** artifact research, digital curation, public presentation.

**Currently enacted:** the opening asks which object to spotlight, then supplies an interpretation and source link. The composer asks for a central claim, artifact descriptions, evidence connections, and citations, followed by media/publishing work. The native board displays artifact-to-claim connections and sources. Sample curator first claims, feedback, and revision are explicitly under `teacherView()`.

**Gap:** choosing an attractive object is not yet researching it. The opening does not ask students to distinguish visible features from inferred meaning or consult a source before making a claim. Much of the creative process is represented by writing fields or media setup; the worked revision is framed as adult-only guidance in the fictional sample.

**What to showcase centrally:** rotate/inspect one artifact → mark an observable feature → open a short, credited museum source → choose or write a bounded interpretation → connect a second artifact to the shared claim. A feature-to-source-to-label interaction is the learning experience. Optional help explains how to inspect or cite; the source and inference task stay in the workspace.

**Proof in the final showcase:** select an artifact feature to see the student's evidence connection and source; compare an overbroad draft label with the revised label and reason. Expose this student-safe fictional learning example directly. Real private teacher feedback remains permission-controlled.

### History Live

**Promised:** historical evidence, source perspective, broadcast writing.

**Currently enacted:** the opening asks which lead to follow; its consequence explains the limits of that viewpoint for the student. Later code requires a pitch, usable sources for a reporting date, source-linked claims and passages, uncertainty where flagged, script links, and producer approval. These are substantial learning structures. The sample includes an overreaching initial claim, a producer question, linked sources, and a narrower final report. Native source/review detail is inside disclosures.

**Gap:** the first interaction rehearses selecting a lead without actually weighing the two reports. The final broadcast can be watched without encountering the claim change that makes it educational. Structural validation verifies links and response presence, not historical truth or quality of inference; producer review still matters.

**What to showcase centrally:** compare two short attributed reports → mark what is observation, claim, or interpretation → select a supporting/challenging passage → rewrite the headline or script line to match the evidence. Keep reporting date and source identity visible. Avoid assigning a “right” political side as the learning outcome.

**Proof in the final showcase:** a broadcast cue paired with its source, the first wording, the editorial question, and the revision. Show at least one of these transformations in the normal student viewing sequence, with the full sources available alongside it.

### Debate — The Fate of the Republic

**Promised:** historical argument, evidence-based rebuttal, civic discourse.

**Currently enacted:** the opening offers two prewritten challenges after the speeches. Selecting one reveals a prepared consequence; the student does not identify the exact claim or formulate the challenge. Main work requires listening/reading, at least one annotation when responding to an opponent, selected evidence, a draft, a reasoning contribution, rehearsal, and recording readiness. The premiere contains filed opposing arguments, evidence, and aggregate votes.

**Gap:** a prewritten challenge is a useful modeled question, but the opening stops before the learner practices rebuttal. Required main steps can be obscured by chamber navigation. Filing checks show participation/completeness, not that a response fairly represents and answers the opponent. Persuasion votes do not demonstrate individual learning by themselves.

**What to showcase centrally:** hear/read a short claim → mark its exact words → paraphrase it fairly → choose a relevant source → construct a response → acknowledge what the opposing argument still establishes. Allow text and recorded responses according to the task's requirements.

**Proof in the final showcase:** opponent's claim, student's marked passage, supporting source, and actual rebuttal together; then show the later claim change. Class voting remains part of the civic event but does not replace this evidence of reasoning.

### Journey — Race Around the World

**Promised:** geographic reasoning, source perspective, evidence-based decisions.

**Currently enacted:** the opening offers a direct passage or supply-harbor route and supplies their consequences. Main steps collect explanations and citations to allowed source paragraphs. Records preserve resources, selected/viewed evidence, responses, and revisions. The replay shows student reasoning; the crisis sample has an actual earlier answer and revision. Browser inspection found the prediction/consequence in the Captain's Log disclosure, with exact sources and revision behind further disclosures.

**Gap:** the opening asks for route selection without eliciting a source-based comparison. The main explanation step includes a prediction field, but `assertResponse()` checks response/citation structure and does not require prediction. Thus displaying that field does not prove the student predicted before observing a consequence. The replay's strongest learning sequence is secondary to the route animation and final response.

**What to showcase centrally:** compare candidate routes on the map with time/supply/source information → make a prediction before the relevant consequence is revealed → choose and justify → inspect the resource change → revise or defend the decision using the evidence. Students should use the map to reason, not simply watch a ship move.

**Proof in the final showcase:** predicted tradeoff versus recorded consequence with the cited map/source and a before/after explanation. The existing crisis sample supports this; promote it into a normal learning scene rather than nesting it under the log. Retain the existing distinction between evidence collected and teacher-assessed mastery.

### Story — Survival Island Story Lab

**Promised:** narrative craft, character and consequence, revision through playtesting.

**Currently enacted:** the opening chooses a historical setting and receives a prepared story opportunity. Main planning asks for original student ideas; the writer collects scenes and reader choices. Readiness requires story fields, minimum scene length, distinct choice labels, and playtests reaching at least two different endings. Revision checkpoints exist. The local coach supplies contextual questions and generic prompts; it does not evaluate continuity or narrative quality. The sample provides a playable completed story.

**Gap:** selecting a setting does not yet showcase writing craft. Reaching two endings is not evidence that the student identified and repaired a story problem. Readiness requires neither a playtest observation nor a revision tied to it. The sample guide describes an early ending and revision, but the sample scene factory sets `revisions: []`; the reader sees a finished story and author note rather than inspectable draft changes.

**What to showcase centrally:** compose a short scene with two meaningful reader choices → play a path → record what the reader expected and what actually followed → revise the confusing sentence/choice, or justify keeping it → replay that part. Keep the character's goal and the cost of each option evident through the scene, not explanatory coach copy.

**Proof in the final showcase:** playable story plus a selected actual draft/revision pair, the playtest observation, and how the change improves a consequence. A word count or second ending badge cannot stand in for that work. Fictional sample revisions should be stored as explicit sample records and labeled accordingly.

## How the showcase should teach

Preserve the two-header navigation plan. Within the central workspace, the current learning task must be unmistakable. A source excerpt, calculation, test comparison, argument, or scene is primary content; tutorials about using the interface are optional help.

Each project should offer a brief **Try it** experience and a **See an example** experience through the project header. These can reuse the same activity/presentation components with isolated practice state. Do not add another dashboard or force the same worksheet across disciplines.

- **Try it:** the viewer performs one representative intellectual action using the real tools. Give useful feedback and a chance to improve; retain practice work separately from official submissions.
- **See an example:** show a concrete problem, an initial attempt, relevant evidence, feedback or consequence, and the changed result. Let the viewer inspect the actual sample record. Show the finished product in context of how it was made.
- **Help:** open a short model, word explanation, replay, or scaffold beside the task. A guide assists the learner's operation; it does not silently perform it.

The full sequence need not remain on screen at once. The project header can show short step labels; the center shows only the active work. One main learning operation at a time keeps the experience simple without removing its intellectual substance.

## Required evidence and feedback changes

Use existing runtime events, drafts, records, and adapters wherever possible. Distinguish student work from configured/system-generated output. At a bounded checkpoint retain the task/version and presented values, student response or representation, feedback/support used, revision, and resulting artifact/evidence link. Do not log every keystroke.

For objective arithmetic, check the result and relevant units using the existing domain arithmetic; support inspecting intermediate work or a mathematical representation. For interpretation and creative work, use task-specific structural checks and human review where appropriate. Do not present character counts, number of sources, completion, eventual correctness, or class votes as proof of reasoning quality.

Keep demonstration completion, practice attempted, supported completion, and assessed performance distinguishable. Skipping an opening may remain a legitimate route to the main project, but it must not create evidence that the omitted skill was practiced. Reading a worked example should not be labeled as an independent student solution.

`TEMPLATE_CAPABILITY_GAP` — the current shared opening renderer/receipt supports choice and supplied consequence, not a student-authored skill attempt with feedback and revision. The smallest reusable fix is an optional registered practice-task capability or adapter to an existing activity, with a typed evidence receipt and validation. Check existing activity hosts/contracts before extending the opening schema. Do not add seven bespoke pedagogical engines or make future AI a dependency.

6. **P2: Feedback and evidence completeness.** Improve arithmetic step feedback and bounded attempt/support records; audit every completion label against what it actually proves.

Implementation checks:

- Complete every opening as a student. Name the skill practiced and point to the student-produced evidence. Merely pressing Play/Continue or receiving a generated result does not satisfy this check.
- In Trading, attempt wrong and correct cost/profit answers; check that premature result badges do not leak the target answer, feedback is useful, and the receipt distinguishes attempts from system totals. Exercise all three cargo pairs and verify consistent item/bundle language.
- Confirm every project's required evidence/source is directly available at the decision point, with readable labels and no search through unrelated navigation.
- In each final example, locate one genuine sample attempt → feedback/consequence → revision path. Verify that displayed changes correspond to stored sample records.
- Check Story's playtest-to-revision link and Journey's before-consequence prediction timing rather than testing only field presence.
- Test return/refresh, draft retention, help focus, and isolation of practice from official work. Run relevant component/domain tests, build, and architecture checks after implementation.
- Observe students at the intended grade level: can they find the task, perform the target operation, use feedback, and explain what changed? Capture both navigation difficulty and conceptual difficulty; a cleaner screenshot alone is insufficient.
