## 1. What needs rebuilding

The current package contains one historical repair: premature potato cargo at a
European harbor in 1490. Eight sessions reuse that case through inspection,
ordering, source selection, written ripple comparisons, and exhibit captions.
Weeks 2–4 rely heavily on text; the final asks for text authoring. The workspace
also adds its own heading, session navigation, instruction block, and footer.

Keep the idea of investigating and repairing an unreliable historical record.
Replace the cargo curriculum and its eight-session progression. Build four
substantial historical problems whose interactions reveal something worth
discussing. Visual asset reuse must not determine the new history curriculum.

## 2. Historical focus and purpose

**Central question:** How does knowledge survive, get tested, and reach new people?

**Connecting idea:** People developed ways to observe and represent the world,
then preserved, translated, adapted, and reproduced that knowledge. Making an
accurate copy, testing an explanation, applying an idea, and making many copies
are different kinds of work.

This fits the supplied Grade 7 unit **Ideas That Changed the World** and its
**Power and Networks Tracker**. That unit names knowledge preservation,
translation, libraries, Islamic scholarship, the Renaissance, humanism, art,
science, printing, and information access. Use the grade placement as a planning
assumption, not a claim of verified standards coverage: the repository has no
supplied Forge Grade 7 standards crosswalk.

### What students should understand by the end

1. Scholars in the medieval Islamic world preserved, translated, criticized,
   and extended knowledge. They were active investigators, not merely custodians
   of someone else's achievements.
2. Observations and repeatable tests can challenge a respected explanation.
3. Renaissance workshops used geometry, observation, and older learning to
   develop particular ways of representing space.
4. Printing changed the production and circulation of knowledge. A repeated
   error can spread along with a repeated idea.
5. Translators, artisans, patrons, institutions, materials, language, and readers
   all affect which ideas travel and who can use them.
6. A surviving object has a date and a history of copying and ownership; its date
   does not automatically date the original idea.

These are history goals. The geometry and optics give students concrete evidence
to work with; mechanical success alone cannot demonstrate historical understanding.

## 3. The time-repair premise

A future archive reconstructs historical workplaces from surviving evidence.
Four records have been corrupted: a copied diagram, an experiment, a workshop
demonstration, and a printed edition. The student enters each reconstruction and
uses its tools to find the contradiction and restore a defensible record.

The archive can be repaired; the student does not become the inventor of someone
else's work. Historical people retain credit. The four visits are a teaching
sequence, not a claim that one original manuscript passed through every room or
that history followed a single inevitable path to Europe.

Keep the fiction light: one concise mission in the task box. Each session opens
with the relevant object already in reach. Discovery and changing objects carry
the experience; there is no cutscene prerequisite, countdown, or repeated briefing.

### Approaches considered

| Approach | Decision |
| --- | --- |
| Spot modern objects in historical paintings | Useful for a short introduction, too shallow for the core unit. |
| Rescue a famous inventor and restore the future | Reject: makes complex historical change depend on a single hero and encourages invented causal claims. |
| Repair working demonstrations and trace the people behind them | Recommended: supports distinct interactions, historical inquiry, and a cumulative final investigation. |

## 4. Four weeks, eight sessions

### Week 1 — The unreliable manuscript

**Context:** A reconstructed scholarly workspace in Abbasid Baghdad, approximately
the ninth century. Baghdad was a major center of study; scholars working in
Arabic drew on Greek, Persian, Indian, and other traditions and developed new
observations and models. Introduce the House of Wisdom within this wider network,
without claiming to reproduce a documented room or its exact working practices.
[Historical basis: The Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/astronomy-and-astrology-in-the-medieval-islamic-world).

**History question:** What work is required to preserve knowledge across people,
languages, and generations?

**Session 1: Find the copy that cannot work.**

- The work surface holds three teaching reconstructions of a geometric diagram.
  They resemble related manuscripts but disagree on a line or construction.
- A pivoting compass and straightedge let the student reproduce the construction
  on a small board, then overlay each diagram on the result.
- The plausible-looking error becomes visible when the construction fails to
  meet its own constraints. Correctness depends on the geometry, not matching
  torn-paper edges or a hidden answer color.
- The student can compare and revise without losing the other copies.

**Session 2: Restore the record without erasing its history.**

- A fresh copy contains both a damaged construction and a useful later annotation.
  The student decides which marks to restore, which to retain as an addition, and
  which uncertainty to leave open, using physical overlays and provenance tabs.
- Short source context in the tutor area identifies what the author, translator,
  and later reader contributed. The repaired page keeps those contributions
  distinct. Students do not translate an unfamiliar script by guessing.
- The page becomes usable, while its visible layers preserve the difference
  between copying and changing an idea.

**Weekly task/product:** A tested diagram with a source trail and one retained
uncertainty. The system records selected copies, tests, and repairs automatically.

**Teacher/future tutor checks:** Who did the preserving and translating? What is
evidence of an error? Is the oldest surviving copy necessarily the first version?
Why does a later annotation deserve examination rather than automatic deletion?

**Power and networks:** Libraries, patrons, translators, access to materials and
languages. Explain institutions as well as named scholars.

**Content still to select:** A source-supported geometric construction and
specific manuscript witnesses. Do not invent a historic copying incident or
claim that the teaching diagram is a facsimile. The puzzle can be newly authored;
its historical framing and cited comparisons must remain accurate.

### Week 2 — The room of light

**Context:** A reconstructed Cairo optics workspace in the early eleventh century,
connected to Ibn al-Haytham's study of light. His work combined mathematical
reasoning and experiments; its later translation provides a documented example
of knowledge traveling. [Historical basis: UNESCO's account of Ibn al-Haytham](https://courier.unesco.org/en/articles/ibn-al-haythams-scientific-method).

**History question:** When should observation make someone reconsider an explanation?

**Session 3: Make the hidden image appear.**

- The main panel is a dark chamber with an aperture, movable screen, shutters,
  and a lit scene outside. Initially the reconstruction gives an inconsistent
  result.
- The student closes unwanted light openings, changes the screen position, and
  isolates light paths until an inverted projection becomes visible.
- The screen changes continuously with the geometry. Shutters block the light
  they actually intersect. A small diagnostic ray overlay is available on the
  instrument, rather than a page of controls.
- This is an aperture demonstration, not a modern camera with lenses inserted
  into an eleventh-century scene.

**Session 4: Design a test that distinguishes two explanations.**

- Several independently covered light sources create identifiable points on the
  screen. The student changes one source or blocker at a time and compares the
  resulting projections.
- Two proposed light-path diagrams can be overlaid on the apparatus. One cannot
  account for the observed changes. The student replaces the unsupported part
  of the archive model, then tests an unfamiliar arrangement.
- Trial traces remain available for comparison. Guessing a diagram without
  investigating produces no evidence of why it works.

**Weekly task/product:** A working chamber and a short set of discriminating
tests, captured from actual manipulations.

**Teacher/future tutor checks:** What did the test reveal? What was held constant?
How did Ibn al-Haytham build on and question earlier learning? Why is the visible
simulation a teaching model rather than proof of a historical event?

**Power and networks:** Scholarship in Cairo; intellectual authority; the work
of preserving and translating research. Avoid calling one person the inventor
of all science or all experimentation.

### Week 3 — The impossible courtyard

**Context:** A reconstructed Florentine artist's workshop around the 1430s.
Linear perspective and geometric methods became important in Renaissance
workshops. Brunelleschi demonstrated perspective; Alberti described its principles
in writing. [Historical basis: The Met's Renaissance architecture essay](https://www.metmuseum.org/es/essays/architecture-in-renaissance-italy).

**History question:** How can an older mathematical idea become a new workshop practice?

**Session 5: Make the drawing agree with the view.**

- Through a fixed viewing frame, the student sees a courtyard. A reconstruction
  of its drawing has conflicting depth lines and proportions.
- The student pins the viewing position, moves the horizon and convergence
  point, and uses guide threads to align a transparent drawing with the courtyard.
- The environment and drawing respond to the same projection. A drawing that
  works from one viewpoint visibly fails when that viewpoint moves.
- Repair concerns this specific geometric demonstration. Other artistic systems
  are not presented as wrong or inferior art.

**Session 6: Build a convincing illusion.**

- A new shallow stage or cabinet contains movable panels and columns. Students
  arrange depth and scale to create a convincing view through a viewing aperture.
- A second position exposes how the illusion was constructed. Students compare
  their plan, the intended viewing position, and the result.
- The source investigation distinguishes a maker's working diagram, a later
  interpretation, and a surviving artwork. The artifact's date remains visible
  in its source information, not inferred from its visual style.

**Weekly task/product:** A reconstructed perspective study and a tested illusion.

**Teacher/future tutor checks:** What did the workshop do with geometry? What
could an illustrated explanation communicate to another maker? What roles did
patrons and workshops play? What does this one case show about Renaissance
learning, and what does it leave out about humanism?

**Power and networks:** Commissioning, workshop training, authors, and audiences.
Piero della Francesca's later illustrated perspective treatise can serve as a
comparison source, clearly dated to the later fifteenth century rather than used
as a prop from the 1430s. [Museo Galileo's treatise exhibition](https://exhibits.museogalileo.it/deprospectivapingendi/index.html).

### Week 4 — The error that multiplied

**Context:** A reconstructed European print workshop, with a final evidence anchor
in Basel, 1572. A surviving Latin printed edition of Ibn al-Haytham's optics gives
the project a real connection back to Week 2. The catalog establishes its
publication, language, and contributors; it does not establish every link in its
transmission. [Historical object: Wellcome Collection's 1572 edition](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/dfcrfesa).

**History question:** What changes when knowledge can be reproduced—and who still
controls access?

**Session 7: Diagnose the repeated error.**

- The press is already assembled. A proof sheet, reusable type, and a separate
  illustration block are within reach. One part of the teaching plate contains
  an error that appears in every impression.
- Students align the block, adjust inking and pressure through the actual
  tools, and pull a proof. Ink or alignment faults change as those controls
  change; a wrong diagram remains wrong across otherwise clean copies.
- They must trace the fault to its cause, use earlier evidence to correct the
  teaching illustration, and verify a new impression. There is no typing task.
- Use an explicitly authored teaching plate beside a genuine reference image.
  Do not fabricate a printing error in the real 1572 edition or imply that all
  pictures were assembled from letter type.

**Session 8: Restore a trustworthy edition.**

- Students receive an unfamiliar mixed case: a missing credit, a cleanly printed
  but incorrect diagram, and a copied detail whose original date is uncertain.
- They inspect the physical proofs and relevant earlier test traces, repair the
  supported faults, retain uncertainty, and bind their reconstructed edition.
- A compact distribution model on the worktable lets them send copies to
  selected readers or institutions. Copied mistakes travel until corrected;
  a copy can arrive without being accessible to that reader because of the
  selected language or reading context. These are transparent teaching rules,
  not reconstructed statistics about actual historical readership.
- The final object opens into the student's four tested artifacts and their
  sources. It is assembled from real activity evidence, with no caption-writing
  screen or form at the end.

**Weekly task/product:** A repaired edition with a traceable record of copies,
experiments, adaptations, and printing. It is the proposed final product.

**Teacher/future tutor checks:** How did printing differ from manuscript copying?
Why doesn't wider circulation guarantee truth or universal access? Whose work
must remain credited? What resembles modern copying and sharing, and where does
that comparison stop?

**Power and networks:** Printers, financing, language, institutions, literacy,
and readers. Discuss constraints on access with specific source support; do not
use an invented numerical literacy simulation as historical data.

**Essential chronology correction:** Introduce Gutenberg's mid-fifteenth-century
European printing context, but do not call it the beginning of printing
everywhere. Compare the Korean *Jikji*, printed with movable metal type in 1377.
[Library of Congress Gutenberg guide](https://guides.loc.gov/gutenberg),
[UNESCO's Jikji record](https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/baegun-hwasang-chorok-buljo-jikji-simche-yojeol-volii-second-volume-anthology-great-buddhist-priests?hub=915).

## 5. How the historical lesson is actually taught

The activity supplies things to inspect and test. Historical context cannot be
assumed to emerge from turning a dial or matching diagrams.

Each session therefore needs these authored teaching ingredients, placed in the
side column or an on-demand source view:

- A brief explanation of the place, period, people, and central historical question.
- One or two carefully selected source objects, with a short readable adaptation
  and exact provenance. Distinguish primary material from museum interpretation
  and from our fictional reconstruction.
- A prompt that connects an actual manipulation to the history, followed by a
  fresh example or comparison that exposes superficial guessing.
- One Power and Networks connection: who enables the work, who can use it, and
  whose contribution the record might omit.

The weekly task box remains directly above **AI Tutor**. During the disconnected
prototype, the tutor box records this lesson content and the intended questions,
evidence, and controls for review. Teacher delivery is needed where teaching is
not yet implemented; a planning checklist must not be reported as a taught or
assessed lesson. Do not fabricate tutor replies or completion decisions.

Avoid a stack of reading assignments. Short source context must support the
current investigation. All written answers remain in the tutor area. No extra
heading, instructions, navigation, footer, or product form surrounds the main panel.

## 6. Content checks before visual production

- Every week has a different interaction and a different historical question.
- Every session requires a meaningful comparison or choice, not merely clicking
  all hotspots or setting every control once.
- At least one source-dependent decision per session prevents the activity from
  becoming an unrelated science or geometry toy.
- Dates of ideas, copies, translations, surviving artifacts, and printed editions
  stay distinct. Later reference images do not silently become earlier props.
- Preserve the contributions of multiple cultures and people. Do not draw an
  unsupported direct line from Baghdad to a named Florentine artist.
- Manuscript annotation, scientific testing, artistic representation, and printing
  are not interchangeable definitions of progress.
- The four-week scope offers selected case studies. It does not claim to cover
  all Islamic history, the Renaissance, humanism, or the Scientific Revolution.
- Source claims and artwork references need verification at the individual object
  level before a historically specific asset is commissioned.

## 7. Feasibility and the next design stage

**Reusable foundation:** Existing lesson routing, task/tutor layout, source
metadata, inspection state, repair trials, scoped drafts, configuration validation,
and direct-entry preview support can be retained where suitable. No student work
should be migrated by silently replacing the old published package.

**TEMPLATE_CAPABILITY_GAP — interactive historical workbenches:** The current
Time Repair renderer swaps an object, orders cards, and changes authored prose.
It does not contain manuscript comparison tools, optical ray geometry,
perspective construction, or a press model. These require bounded reusable
activity capabilities, with configuration, real model responses, validation,
meaningful tests, and accessible controls. They are not implemented yet.

The proposed interactions can be developed as focused 2D/2.5D workbenches using
the existing Angular/TypeScript stack. Prove one mechanism at a time with simple
shapes before committing to finished assets. Do not promise a free-roaming city,
fully simulated historical society, real-time shared classroom, or video pipeline.

**After content review:** Design the composition and interaction states for each
workbench. Establish camera position, working-object scale, clickable areas,
foreground/background separation, and feedback states; then create the needed
art. Generated images must not contain the puzzle's only working controls or bake
in its answers. Motion should come from the working apparatus. No videos are
needed for this concept.

**Review focus:** Confirm the four-week historical arc and the proposed final
repaired edition before advancing to detailed visual and interaction design.
