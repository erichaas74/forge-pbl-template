# Time Repair — four-week history restructure

Current package: `exploration-time-repair@2.3.0`.

The project now teaches a broken timeline as an investigation. The learner is
not told that the press failed at the start. They infer the missing event, trace
the cause through alternate evidence, repair Gutenberg's press in Week 3, and
then compare the repaired model with documented history.

## Four-week arc

| Week | Historical question | Main-panel activity | What the learner discovers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 · Find what did not happen | What event is missing between Luther's manuscript and a quiet 1518? | A broadsheet wall leads with five fictional newspaper headlines. Students inspect four clickable clippings, place candidate events in the gap, and test the candidate against every clue. An interview room lets students hear three fictional alternate-world witnesses and inspect their objects. | Luther's idea exists and can travel by hand; the missing step is reliable printed circulation. |
| 2 · Find what caused the gap | Why would the printed debate fail to appear? | Alternate newspaper issues: inspect two columns in each edition. A second interview room pairs an ink mixer, type setter, and courier with physical evidence from the failed workshop. | The fictional cause is a linked process failure, especially ink that beads on metal type. Newspapers and interviews are evidence to weigh, not proof by themselves. |
| 3 · Repair and learn Gutenberg | Which parts make movable-type printing repeatable? | The only repair week. Students test inks and pressure, then assemble and operate the frame, mold, ink ball, and screw. | Material, type, pressure, and skilled crafts must cooperate. The repair is a qualitative model, not Gutenberg's exact machine. |
| 4 · See the history that happened | What does the surviving record show after the repair? | Historical newspaper-style source cards for Leipzig, Nuremberg, and Basel, followed by a repaired-versus-documented timeline comparison. | Printing helped Luther's debate travel, but institutions, rulers, language, religious argument, conflict, and people also shaped the Protestant Reformation. |

## Activity rules checked

- Every session remains directly accessible during testing. No completion, score,
  tutor, or earlier-session gate exists.
- The main panel is one visual investigation. Timeline, newspaper, and interview
  actions change objects and evidence state; no main-panel text entry or answer
  form is used.
- The repair workbench is used only in Week 3. Investigation weeks do not render
  the press repair screen.
- Task remains directly above the disconnected AI Tutor. Standards stay in the
  compact expandable shell row.
- The alternate timeline is explicitly fictional. The final week uses surviving
  print evidence and does not present a single broken press as the sole cause of
  the Reformation.

## Historical boundaries

The Library of Congress identifies the 1517 Ninety-five Theses as a document
intended for debate, describes its rapid spread through printing, and records
early editions in Leipzig, Nuremberg, and Basel. The project uses that documented
record in Week 4. The missing circulation, alternate newspapers, witnesses, and
counterfactual cause belong to the teaching fiction. Gutenberg's Mainz context is
placed before Luther's controversy; Gutenberg did not print the theses or meet
Luther in this story.

## Implementation

- `timeline`, `newspaper`, and `interview` are reusable knowledge capabilities with
  validated configuration, event replay, local persistence, and keyboard controls.
- Week 3 uses the existing press engine and the reusable assembly activity only.
- Package content is generated by `scripts/configure-four-week-history.mjs` into
  `public/projects/exploration-time-repair/versions/2.3.0/` and mirrored into the
  lesson-plan registry.
- SVG scenes use the existing visual language: layered rooms, paper, witnesses,
  evidence objects, focus rings, visible state changes, and reduced-motion rules.
