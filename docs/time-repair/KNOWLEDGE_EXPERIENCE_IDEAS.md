# Time Repair — knowledge learned through interaction

Brainstorm for user review, 2026-09-15. These are proposed experiences, not
implemented features or an approved eight-session replacement. Current production
still has the previously audited workshop/courtyard activities.

Testing access stays open. Completing tasks or gathering evidence must not lock
access to lessons, scenes, or examples. This corrects the earlier suggestion that
free lesson navigation was a weakness.

## Design direction

The press should change what people can do with knowledge. Build visible chains
from materials to pages to human work. Give the student evidence to interpret,
decisions to try, and consequences worth looking at. Keep tasks and explanations
in the side column. Use object manipulation, comparison lenses, physical puzzles,
and scene changes in the main panel. No typing, quizzes, extra panels, video
placeholders, or decorative inventor who never responds.

All particular customers, workshop accidents, dialogue, and future outcomes are
authored fiction. A diagram used to make information transfer visible is an
illustrative learning prop, not a claimed Gutenberg publication. Models of time,
cost, material behavior, and access are qualitative rather than historical data.

## Candidate experiences

### 1. One book, six worktables

A cutaway learning room contains one reference book and several tables making
small geometric constructions. Move the book, pair people around it, or allocate
a copyist. Teams can only use the part of the diagram they have actually seen.
Partially built objects and waiting hands reveal the constraint. A clock advances
with meaningful actions; students do not sit through timed waits.

Later, replay the same room with usable printed copies. Several tables can now
compare the same diagram. Discover scarcity, shared access, and the difference
between making information available and understanding it. Preserve spoken
teaching and collaboration in both versions of the scene.

### 2. Two matching books with different secrets

Two open volumes sit on a conservation table. Rotate a raking-light lamp and
slide one transparent page over the other. Printed letter shapes and positions
line up, while hand-added initials and ornament differ. Lift a page corner to
inspect another section rather than answering a spot-the-difference quiz.

Discover that early printed books combined repeated impressions with individual
handwork. The Library of Congress documents hand-colored capitals and headings:
https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/bibles/the-gutenberg-bible.html

### 3. The letter that never touches the paper

A cutaway type tray includes one slightly short block. Place a straightedge over
the letters, move a thin contact strip beneath it, and make an impression. The
same missing mark appears in every copy. Recast the virtual block with a corrected
mold stop, return it to the tray, and retest.

Discover that interchangeable pieces need consistent dimensions. This introduces
manufacturing precision rather than repeating the five-letter spelling puzzle.
The adjustable mold is an explicit teaching reconstruction; the exact details of
Gutenberg's casting process are not asserted.

### 4. Gutenberg's wrong guess

The inventor points to the pressure mechanism after a faint impression. Student
and inventor make a paired test: the inventor changes pressure while the student
keeps pressure fixed and changes the ink/material combination. An inspection lens
reveals pooling, broken coverage, or spreading edges. Select a physical proof to
hand back; the inventor lays it beside his result and changes his next action.

Discover controlled comparison and how evidence changes an explanation. Dialogue
is authored and belongs in the companion/tutor area. The core ink incompatibility
and need for consistent pressure have historical support; the wrong guess is
fiction. Anchor: https://www.themorgan.org/collections/works/gutenberg/invention-of-printing

### 5. The beautiful page that stops the workshop

Produce a black impression and align a second red impression using physical
registration stops. Misalignment remains visible on the page. Alternatively,
send the black impression to an artisan for hand finishing. Choose a workflow
for a small set of orders; drying racks, paper use, and waiting work change.

Discover the tradeoff between appearance, repeated setup, materials, and labor.
Gutenberg tried printing red rubrics, then abandoned the additional printing step
because of its time and effort. The workshop scheduling game is a simplified
interpretation of that documented choice, not a reconstruction of its costs.
Anchor: https://www.themorgan.org/collections/works/gutenberg/invention-of-printing

### 6. Read the machine through its mistakes

Several failed sheets hang above a partly concealed press. A magnifier and
transparent alignment overlay help the student compare patterns: a repeated gap,
a faint edge, or spreading marks. Open relevant parts of the press to inspect
contact, type, or ink. Repairs change only the symptom caused by that part.

Discover diagnosis from evidence. Offer plausible competing causes and preserve
failed sheets, rather than immediately naming the faulty component. Distinct
symptoms must come from the mechanism, not arbitrary matching rules.

### 7. The order that challenges a successful repair

A repaired press receives a visibly different paper stock. Its thickness changes
contact with the type. Students inspect the edge, adjust support, and test. Later
a visibly shifted securing wedge produces movement in the impression. They can
trace the change and correct it rather than guessing a hidden random failure.

Discover why one clean result does not prove a process works under different
conditions. The challenge advances by changed conditions and meaningful decisions,
not three identical pulls or punitive random events. Paper behavior and securing
mechanics require a reviewed qualitative model before implementation.

### 8. A mistake travels farther than its maker

A fictional diagram contains a reversed piece. The student prints and distributes
copies, then visits the worktables from experience 1. Every group working from
the bad copy encounters the same construction problem. Correct the master and
dispatch replacement sheets; copies already delivered remain wrong until replaced.

Discover that reproduction spreads errors as well as useful knowledge, and that
repairing a source does not automatically correct everything already circulated.
Return to the same room with the repaired information and watch work resume.
The diagram and resulting activity are fictional props for understanding the
mechanism of information transfer, not a documented Gutenberg episode.

## Additional alternative: the paper shortage

A layout puzzle asks the student to fit a fixed amount of content into fewer
sheets using shorter type bodies and spacing pieces. Overcrowding produces a
visibly unusable page. Compare readability with the paper stack consumed.
This draws on the Gutenberg Bible's shift from 40 to 42 lines described by the
Morgan. It is an alternative to experience 5, not another required session.

## Selection and feasibility

The strongest narrative backbone is 1 → 4 → 6 → 7 → 8: experience the loss,
collaborate on a diagnosis, repair, test changed conditions, revisit consequences.
Experiences 2, 3, and 5 add historical craft, manufacturing, and production choices.
Do not force one idea into each session before pacing and scope are reviewed.

Existing proof, ink, packing, scoped-save, and courtyard systems provide partial
foundations for 4, 6, and 7. The learning-room simulation, page-overlay examination,
mold/contact model, registration/workflow model, inventor response system, and
copy-version distribution would require new reusable capabilities. SVG/canvas
and semantic object controls are suitable; none of these ideas depends on video
generation or connected AI. Artwork follows the chosen mechanism and must show
the evidence and consequences clearly.
