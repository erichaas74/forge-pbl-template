# Time Repair: Inventions at the Breaking Point

## Revised content direction — for review, 2026-09-15

The user's direction is now the central design rule:

**Study an invention that changed history. Introduce a believable reason it fails
or never gets built. Show the world shortly afterward without its contribution.
Send the student back to help the inventor discover and correct the problem.
Return to the later world and inspect what changes.**

This replaces the earlier archive-restoration proposal. The student repairs a
working invention inside an alternate-history story. Historical investigation,
mechanical diagnosis, and the consequences of change belong in the same mission.

The new title, first case, and candidate inventions below are proposals. No app
code, project configuration, art, or video is changed by this document.

## 1. The repeatable mission structure

1. **Understand the invention.** Examine a working reference or reconstruction,
   operate its essential parts, and see what task it makes possible. Introduce
   its real people, place, date range, predecessors, and purpose through the
   side-column lesson content.
2. **Enter the failed future.** Visit a small, explorable scene months or a few
   years after the fictional failure. Investigate visible consequences in daily
   work. The scene presents a problem worth traveling back to solve.
3. **Find the turning point.** Trace a failed product, missing delivery, or stalled
   process to the inventor's workshop. The inventor has a plausible hypothesis;
   the student helps test it instead of receiving the answer.
4. **Diagnose and repair.** Inspect symptoms, compare trials, isolate the cause,
   change the relevant parts or materials, and test the whole mechanism.
5. **Prove the invention works.** It must perform its actual useful task across
   more than one trial. A correct multiple-choice answer cannot repair it.
6. **Return and inspect the difference.** Revisit the same people and locations.
   Show the particular bottleneck that has eased, and what still has not changed.
7. **Explain the history.** In the tutor area, connect the mechanism, its effects,
   and the evidence. Distinguish the real invention from our fictional fault and
   imagined outcome.

Use the same scene positions in the two futures so that changes can be discovered
directly. The world's response is tied to the working model: usable products,
delivered signals, or changed material conditions. Do not substitute before/after
paragraph cards, a completion screen, or a prerecorded movie.

## 2. First proposed rescue — The Press That Never Printed

### Historical anchor

Gutenberg and his collaborators produced the Gutenberg Bible in Mainz in the
mid-1450s. The project involved specialized printing processes and substantial
financial support. Our scenario concerns this workshop's development of European
printing with movable metal type, not the invention of every form of printing.
[Library of Congress: Gutenberg Bible guide](https://guides.loc.gov/gutenberg).

The Morgan identifies a real engineering challenge: ordinary water-based
manuscript ink did not adhere adequately to metal type. Consistent pressure was
also essential; the exact details of Gutenberg's equipment are not all known.
This gives us a historical basis for the diagnostic problem, without claiming
that our particular accident happened.
[The Morgan: The Invention of Printing](https://www.themorgan.org/collections/works/gutenberg/invention-of-printing).

### The fictional break

**Mainz, around 1454:** A prepared ink batch is spoiled. A replacement is made
with a familiar manuscript-ink binder that performs badly on metal type. The
press makes patchy impressions. Increasing pressure does not solve the underlying
problem. After repeated failed demonstrations, the workshop loses the support
needed to continue.

This accident, failed demonstration, and resulting closure are authored fiction.
They must not be confused with documented events or Gutenberg's actual financial
disputes. The mechanical incompatibility is the researched historical anchor.

### The failed future — a fictional bookseller's courtyard, around 1460

The learner encounters a specific local problem: several customers need copies
of the same work, but there is only one available manuscript and copying takes
time. Students can inspect the courtyard's working processes:

- A scribe advances through one manuscript while unfinished commissions remain.
- A binder has covers prepared but is waiting for their pages.
- A messenger leaves without the requested copy.
- A patron can use the available book while another reader must wait.

This is a deliberately bounded alternate future. Manuscripts, oral teaching,
other printing traditions, and other inventors still exist. We show a possible
local delay in access and production, not proof that all books, schools, or future
science vanish when one workshop fails. The date is a scenario horizon, not a
prediction of how long invention would actually be delayed.

### What the student does in the workshop

The main panel contains a large, operable press and its immediate workbench.
The inventor and assistants react through their work: examining failed sheets,
preparing another impression, comparing a successful batch. Any dialogue is
clearly authored fiction and belongs in the tutor/companion area rather than
covering the mechanism.

**Investigation A — What do the failed sheets tell us?**

- Pull a proof from the malfunctioning press.
- Use a sliding inspection lens to compare the impression with the metal type.
- Try the workshop's hypothesis that stronger pressure will solve the problem.
  The model should show the limited effect of that adjustment while the patchy
  transfer remains.
- Compare ink behavior on a manuscript sample and on the metal test surface.
  The difference supplies a clue about the material relationship.

**Investigation B — Find a usable formulation.**

- Use a small set of historically informed, preconfigured virtual ink samples.
  Test them on the same metal surface under the same press conditions.
- Vary one factor at a time. A formulation that transfers poorly, one that
  spreads excessively in our model, and one that makes a usable impression
  produce different visible results.
- Preserve the proofs so students can compare the results without a written
  experiment form. The final ingredient choices and response model need
  technical/source review; do not invent Gutenberg's secret recipe.

**Investigation C — A clean proof is only the beginning.**

- Install the successful ink choice and produce several impressions.
- Check that the same underlying type can make multiple usable copies.
- Introduce a separate, clearly detectable setup fault in the second session,
  such as uneven packing that affects one region of the impression. Students
  must use the different symptom to diagnose pressure distribution, rather
  than apply the ink fix to every problem.
- Completion of the mechanical task requires a consistent batch under the
  specified test conditions. The tutor separately checks the reasoning.

The exact physical controls should be designed around ink transfer, contact,
alignment, and pressure. No arbitrary lock code, unrelated matching puzzle,
typing task, or magical repair button stands in for operating the press.

### Return to the same courtyard

The same binder now receives usable printed sheets. A messenger can collect a
copy from a batch. More of the waiting commissions can be supplied. Hand finishing
and manuscript work remain visible: early printed books still involved other
artisans, and different copies could be finished differently.
[Library of Congress: production and hand finishing](https://guides.loc.gov/gutenberg).

The model does not make every person literate or every book affordable. Students
can inspect a remaining access barrier and discuss it in the tutor box. The
historical lesson concerns how a production process affects copying, circulation,
labor, investment, and access—not simply that a new machine makes everything good.

### Proposed learning goals and evidence

| History goal | Activity evidence | Tutor/teacher follow-through |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explain how reusable type changes copying | Several impressions from one setup; comparison with hand copying | What process became repeatable, and what work remained? |
| Recognize invention as a system of materials, skills, people, and support | Failed pressure test, ink comparison, successful batch | Why was a working press frame alone insufficient? Who contributed? |
| Explain a short-term consequence through a concrete causal chain | Workshop output connected to the courtyard's unfinished orders | Which observed change follows from increased usable copies? |
| Distinguish history from a counterfactual | Real source objects beside clearly identified scenario records | Which facts are documented? Which events did this story invent? |
| Recognize limits on access and claims of priority | A remaining barrier after the repair; an earlier printing reference | Who could still be excluded? What existed before this European workshop? |

For the earlier-printing comparison, use a short source reference to Korea's
*Jikji*, printed with movable metal type in 1377. It should enrich the invention's
history without becoming a second required repair mission.
[UNESCO: Jikji](https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/baegun-hwasang-chorok-buljo-jikji-simche-yojeol-volii-second-volume-anthology-great-buddhist-priests?hub=915).

## 3. Other promising rescue cases

These are candidates if the user chooses a different invention each week. They
are not an approved four-week sequence. Industrial-era choices would broaden
the earlier medieval/Renaissance content scope and need a fresh curriculum map.

| Candidate | Authored mechanical failure | Possible near-term alternate scene | Student's repair |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Galileo's improved telescope, 1609–1610 | A replacement spacer prevents a usable lens separation | In 1611, this workshop lacks the clear observations and published evidence it otherwise contributed; the sky itself is unchanged | Test lenses on a distant target, restore alignment/separation, and use repeated observations to distinguish moving moons from fixed points |
| Newcomen's atmospheric pumping engine, 1712 | The cooling-water inlet is blocked, preventing effective condensation | A nearby mine in 1713 still has water above its intended working level and relies on its earlier pumping arrangements | Trace the cycle, restore the cooling-water supply, observe the piston and pump respond, and lower the water level |
| Morse and Vail's telegraph demonstration, 1844 | A fault in the line insulation causes the distant receiver to fail despite a working local test | The following year, messages on this route still travel with a messenger; recipients must wait for the journey | Compare local and distant circuits, isolate the line fault, restore insulation, and deliver a supplied signal pattern to the distant register |

### Historical anchors for candidate selection

- **Telescope:** Galileo developed improved telescopes after Dutch examples.
  Credit that prior work. The surviving instruments and accounts support the
  lens-based activity; the spacer fault is fiction.
  [Museo Galileo: Telescope](https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/multimedia/TelescopeBis.html).
- **Observation:** Jupiter's moons provided evidence of bodies orbiting something
  other than Earth. Do not turn this into an instant, universal acceptance of
  heliocentrism.
  [Museo Galileo: Jupiter's moons](https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/indepth/JupitersMoonsSatellites.html).
- **Steam pump:** The Newcomen engine condensed steam to form a partial vacuum;
  atmospheric pressure drove the piston. Its first installation near Dudley was
  in 1712. Do not depict it as a railway locomotive or a high-pressure engine.
  [Science Museum Group: Newcomen engine](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co50900/newcomen-atmospheric-engine).
- **Telegraph:** The Washington–Baltimore demonstration took place in 1844;
  technical collaborators and financial support were essential. Earlier
  electrical and visual signaling systems belong in the historical context.
  [Library of Congress: Invention of the Telegraph](https://www.loc.gov/collections/samuel-morse-papers/articles-and-essays/invention-of-the-telegraph/).

## 4. Presentation rules for this new premise

- The main panel is the current explorable scene or working invention. No new
  headers, footers, menus, instruction strips, or controls outside its bounds.
- The weekly task box stays above the AI Tutor box. Place travel actions in the
  task area or existing shared shell; do not add a scene-navigation bar.
- The invention's useful actions belong directly on its parts or tools. The
  physical feedback should be observable without a paragraph describing it.
- Study notes, source details, fictional dialogue, written responses, and
  historical questions belong in the side area or its contained disclosures.
- The tutor remains disconnected until separately implemented. A checklist of
  intended teaching and evidence is not a functioning teacher or assessor.
- The time transition and future changes should be programmed scene states.
  This proposal requires no videos and makes no claim of generated video.
- Counterfactual scenes must be identified as authored possibilities in the
  task/tutor context. A simulated result is not evidence that history could only
  have developed that way.
- Fixes must change behavior. Replacing a background and showing a congratulatory
  label is insufficient.

## 5. Scope awaiting the user's choice

An optional question is pending: **one invention across four weeks, or a different
invention each week?** The first press case can support either structure, so its
mechanical and historical design can be reviewed now without choosing the rest.

- **If one invention spans the project:** develop four substantial investigations
  around its purpose and failed future, reusable type, ink/contact/presswork,
  and reliable production with a return to the later world. Each week still
  needs a distinct interactive problem, not a repeated press with a new heading.
- **If each week rescues an invention:** use individual investigation in the first
  session and collaborative repair/transfer in the second. Keep each mechanism
  bounded enough to investigate and test within those two sessions. Shared
  editing remains future work.

## 6. Implementation boundary and next step

The existing Time Repair engine does not simulate these machines or their
near-term world states. **TEMPLATE_CAPABILITY_GAP:** configurable invention
workbenches and linked consequence scenes are required. Each needs explicit
state, fault definitions, observable responses, trial capture, accessibility,
validation, and tests. This is content planning, not a claim those capabilities
already exist.

After the user reviews the mission and weekly scope, design the actual scene
composition and interactions. Prove the press's diagnostic behavior, then
commission the workshop and courtyard art around it. Preserve the current
published project until a separately versioned replacement is ready.
