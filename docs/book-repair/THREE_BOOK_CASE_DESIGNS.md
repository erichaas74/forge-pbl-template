# Cinematic Book Repair: three book designs

Design specification, 2026-09-15. Three candidate book packages for the separate Book Repair project: **Frindle**, **The Westing Game**, and **The Phantom Tollbooth**. This document adds one developed starter case and two expansion concepts per book. It does not select a production pilot or implement an app, engine feature, image, or video.

Companions: [cinematic direction](CINEMATIC_BOOK_REPAIR.md), [implementation specification](IMPLEMENTATION_SPECIFICATION.md), and [case authoring template](CASE_AUTHORING_TEMPLATE.md). The newer [project activity rules](../PROJECT_ACTIVITY_RULES.md) govern presentation wherever the earlier documents differ. History Time Repair and other curriculum projects are outside this design.

## The experience shared by all three

**Watch a wrong event → enter the same 3D location shortly before it happens → interview characters and inspect evidence → repair the interference → return to watch the book's event restored.**

The student is a visiting reader who can intervene in this altered version of the story. Characters respond to that visitor naturally; they do not know that they are in a novel. The student must distinguish what the author wrote from what this activity has introduced.

Each main case is a proposed 20–30 minute investigation, following the relevant assigned reading. Timing is a planning estimate, not measured lesson performance. The opening and ending are planned prerecorded cinematics. The student moves through actual 3D space between four or five composed camera positions. They can look around, inspect objects, play evidence, choose interview topics, and present objects or passage references. Navigation has no reflex requirement.

The common page shell supplies the project identity and navigation. The activity occupies the main panel, without additional content bands or an instruction dashboard. A character's short topic controls operate the conversation; they are not written response fields. Essential dialogue has captions and an on-demand transcript. Written explanations and proposed assessment questions belong in the separate Tutor area, which remains explicitly disconnected. The current week's tasks and intended products sit immediately above that area.

The three cases deliberately use different investigative actions:

| Book | Student investigates | Main physical interaction | Tone |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Frindle | How an embarrassing rehearsal became a news report | Inspect recordings and restore the correct tape to its case | Warm, funny, increasingly awkward |
| The Westing Game | Whether a decisive word was heard or merely assumed | Examine an altered transcript and correct the record with its author | Quiet, tense, intellectually satisfying |
| The Phantom Tollbooth | Why Milo cannot recognize a trap he originally escaped | Test absurd work, recover a concealed tool, return it to Milo | Lush fantasy, absurd comedy, an unsettling villain |

### Clarity without a worksheet

At every stage, the environment provides a next action and a visible response. A witness may point at an object; that object receives an adjacent interaction label. An optional focus command identifies all reachable interactions, with keyboard and touch equivalents. It does not highlight only the correct answer.

The student can always revisit witnesses and evidence. Completing every dialogue branch is unnecessary. Wrong interventions produce a short, specific local consequence, then allow revision. A saved success is tied to that exact repair trial; changing the repaired object invalidates the current ending until the new state is checked.

The rules recognize authored evidence and installed changes. They do not claim to read the student's mind or grade understanding. The final visual comparison confirms what happened in the scene. Any written interpretation remains a separate learning product.

## 1. Frindle — The Wrong Tape

### The original event and the invented change

**Suggested audience:** grade 5, with grade 6 use possible. **Reading boundary:** through Chapter 12, “Airwaves.” **Case ID proposal:** `frindle-wrong-tape`.

In the novel, television reporter Alice Lunderson interviews Nick at the Allens' home. His parents sit with him, and he explains the origin of his new word. The reporting helps spread the word. This is the event the case restores. See [Chapter 12 background](https://www.litcharts.com/lit/frindle/chapter-12-airwaves) and the [publisher's premise](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Frindle/Andrew-Clements/9780689806698).

For the activity, add a silly camera rehearsal in which Nick pretends that “frindle” means an enormous burp. Two similar tape cases become mixed up after filming. Alice uses the rehearsal as if it were his real explanation. The rehearsal, cassette mix-up, playback equipment, and investigation dialogue are inventions for this case, not events from the book.

**Causal chain:** swapped cases → Alice trusts the case label → the rehearsal is selected → the report gives the wrong meaning → Nick's opportunity to explain his idea becomes public embarrassment.

The case asks how the error happened, not which person is secretly evil. The two recordings and Alice's account establish an accidental packing mistake. They do not justify accusing Mrs. Granger or a classmate of sabotage.

### Opening film: approximately 30 seconds

1. A television glows in the Allens' living room. The report introduces Nick's invention.
2. On screen, Nick's rehearsal performance confidently defines the word as an enormous burp. A brief ridiculous sound follows. The news edit treats the performance as sincere.
3. The camera reveals the real Nick watching, mortified, a pen still in his hand. His parents exchange a puzzled look.
4. The shot settles beside the television and the crew's equipment table. The student can replay the clip or enter the scene.

On entry, a brief temporal transition establishes **“Earlier — before the report was sent.”** The same camera moves away from the television into the room. Alice is reviewing material before departure. This onsite review is part of the authored investigation setup. The report is not a live broadcast that the student must somehow undo without explanation.

### The 3D set

One finished living room with a small entry area. Late afternoon window light, a sofa with visible fabric, scuffed school shoes, the weight of a broadcast camera, and warm practical lamps. The room should feel lived in, not like a generic classroom.

| Position | Meaningful action |
| --- | --- |
| Sofa | Interview Nick; inspect the pen he uses to demonstrate his meaning |
| Equipment table | Pick up and rotate the two tapes and their cases; compare identities |
| Playback monitor | Insert either tape, play, pause, and pin a frame with its audio/transcript |
| Alice's chair | Ask about the report and present a recording that contradicts its label |
| Television | Preview the currently prepared segment; later watch the corrected result |

The parents are film performers and ambient presences, not extra required interviews. Exterior spaces remain visible through windows but are outside the investigation. All essential controls have non-drag alternatives.

### Interviews that change what the student knows

All example lines below are original activity dialogue, not quotations from Clements.

| Witness | Initial topic and response | Evidence follow-up | Unique contribution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nick | **The recording:** “We tried the camera before the real questions. I was being ridiculous.” | Present the clip with his parents: he identifies it as his actual explanation and points out his pen demonstration. | Separates his intention and rehearsal behavior from the statement he wanted published. He does not know what Alice packed. |
| Alice | **The report:** “I used the tape in the interview case. The other case was for practice.” | Present both playback frames and their mismatched case labels: she recognizes the reversal and describes packing the similar cases together. | Explains how the wrong performance entered the report. She did not witness Nick's private intention during practice. |

A question about Mrs. Granger does not unlock a confession. Nick can acknowledge their disagreement; Alice can state that she is working from the recordings. Neither invents knowledge about events beyond Chapter 12.

### What the student does and how the case checks it

| Evidence/action | What it establishes | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assigned-reading reference about Nick's word | The intended meaning is pen | Which physical tape contains the appropriate explanation |
| Play tape A | It contains the ridiculous performance despite its interview label | Whether the performance was intended seriously |
| Play tape B | It contains the explanation with Nick and his parents | How it ended up in the rehearsal case |
| Present the clips to Nick and Alice | Nick confirms intention; Alice explains the labeling mistake | A deliberate saboteur; the evidence does not support one |
| Put the real interview tape in the outgoing case and return it to Alice | The report's source has actually changed | Student mastery of the novel as a whole |

The repair succeeds when the student has established the book's meaning, obtained the two necessary witness facts, identified the recordings by content, and installed the real interview as the outgoing source. Checking labels or collecting objects alone is insufficient. These facts are accumulated in any reasonable order.

Unsuccessful attempts remain useful: simply relabeling the rehearsal leaves the silly video on the monitor; submitting the pen alone makes Alice ask for the recording that shows Nick's explanation; blaming the teacher leads back to the equipment rather than to a new invented villain.

### Corrected film and the reward

Alice loads the verified recording. Return to the opening's living-room composition. This time the television shows Nick explaining his word with a pen in his hand. The camera cuts to his relieved face. The precise restored milestone is an accurate report; the ending does not jump to the later dictionary or financial resolution.

A replay comparison pairs the two broadcast moments. The student sees that fixing the source changed what the audience heard. Their case record retains the recording comparison, witness confirmations, source reference, and installed repair.

### Two expansion concepts

- **The apology that Nick never wrote:** a false statement on the bulletin board makes his friends believe that his word was a trick at their expense. Interview Nick and a friend, examine the notice's route to the board, and establish that it does not reflect his intention before he addresses the group. The notice and reconciliation scene are authored additions; select the precise chapter anchor before production.
- **The missing final letter:** the explanatory letter is absent from Mrs. Granger's dictionary package. Inspect the wrapping and envelope trail to restore the delivery. This case requires Chapter 15 and must retain the book's later time setting. It is about repairing understanding, not manufacturing the dictionary entry. See [Mrs. Granger's role](https://www.litcharts.com/lit/frindle/characters/mrs-granger).

## 2. The Westing Game — The Word That Wasn't There

### The original event and the invented change

**Suggested audience:** grade 6, or supported grade 5 readers. **Reading boundary:** through Chapter 27; assign the entire novel before use if students are not reading together. **Case ID proposal:** `westing-added-word`.

During Turtle's investigation, Sydelle's account matters because an apparently missing word was not actually heard as part of the will. Turtle eventually connects the relevant wording with the fourth identity. The case uses that distinction between a record and an assumption. See [Chapters 25–26](https://www.enotes.com/topics/westing-game/chapter-summaries/chapters-25-26-summary), [the final chapters](https://www.supersummary.com/the-westing-game/chapters-26-30/), and [the chapter sequence](https://www.sparknotes.com/lit/the-westing-game/chapter-summaries/).

For this activity, Sydelle creates an additional plain-language working transcript of her shorthand. Wanting it to look complete, she fills the gap with the word **“murderer”**, based on what people assumed. She later treats that addition as if it were something she recorded. This working transcript and her mistaken amendment are the introduced interference; they must never be presented as Raskin's actual version of Sydelle's actions.

**Causal chain:** assumption inserted into a working transcript → unsupported certainty in testimony → Turtle searches for the wrong thing → the clue to the fourth identity is overlooked → her opportunity to finish the game is lost in this altered version.

The mystery is not whether Sydelle is a murderer. It is whether she heard the disputed word or filled it in afterward. The canonical will remains untouched.

### Opening film: approximately 35 seconds

1. Evening at Judge Ford's apartment. The camera approaches the table where Turtle is reviewing the evidence.
2. Turtle asks about the gap. Sydelle firmly indicates the completed working transcript. The added word seems to close the question.
3. Turtle sets aside the will and turns back toward a row of suspect photographs. The promising line of reasoning has vanished.
4. Hold on the disputed transcript beside the original document. Enter the scene moves back to the minutes before this exchange.

The film's failure is specific: Turtle loses the decisive lead. Do not portray Crow's original arrest as the invented error, announce a false canonical winner, or call Westing a newly exposed murderer.

### The 3D set

An intimate apartment investigation: lake light at the window, warm lamps, worn paper, an upholstered chair, and the quiet sound of a building at night. Noir lighting should preserve readable faces and documents. No threatening chase or action-game interface is needed.

| Position | Meaningful action |
| --- | --- |
| Central table | Inspect the original will beside the working transcript; use raking light and magnification |
| Sydelle's chair | Ask how her record was made and present the disputed insertion |
| Window desk | Interview Turtle about what evidence would change her approach |
| Sideboard | Inspect the shorthand pad and the later working sheet together |
| Table after the repair | Observe Sydelle correct the record and Turtle resume her inference |

The shorthand is a texture and provenance clue, not an expectation that children can decode Polish shorthand. A selectable accessible transcription describes the relevant gap. An untranslated page is never the sole path to progress.

### Interviews

| Witness | Initial position | Evidence follow-up | Knowledge boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sydelle | **The record:** she believes her working sheet is accurate and wants its importance recognized. | Present the matching gap in the original pad and the different writing material on the later sheet. She acknowledges completing it afterward, then distinguishes the interruption she remembers from the words she actually heard. | She knows her own note-making. She does not know the fourth identity and cannot supply it to reward a correct click. |
| Turtle | **The question:** she is treating the completed phrase as a firm instruction. | Present Sydelle's corrected account and the original will. She recognizes that an assumption has constrained the search. | She explains why exact wording matters; she does not announce the answer before the record is corrected. |

Example authored response from Sydelle: “I wanted the copy to be complete. I remembered everyone saying it. That isn't the same as hearing it in the will.” This admission follows visible evidence of the addition. It is not an unexplained final reveal.

### Evidence, intervention, and recognition

| Evidence/action | Role in the solution |
| --- | --- |
| Assigned-reading reference to the interrupted wording and Turtle's reasoning | Establishes the authentic narrative hinge without supplying an accusation |
| Original will inspection | Shows that the disputed addition is not present there |
| Shorthand pad and working sheet comparison | Shows the progression from a gap to a completed phrase; readable provenance accompanies it |
| Raking-light inspection | Reveals that the added word uses a different writing material; this alone proves neither timing nor motive |
| Sydelle's evidence-backed acknowledgment | Establishes when and why she added the word |
| Present corrected testimony to Turtle | Gives Turtle a reason to reopen her interpretation |

**The repair:** hand the working sheet back to Sydelle with the supporting comparison and request correction of the record. She erases only her added pencil word and states the uncertainty accurately. The student then presents the original will and revised account to Turtle. This is a physical handover and performed conversation, not a text-editing form.

The success rule requires the source distinction, Sydelle's acknowledgment, removal of the unsupported addition from the working copy, and presentation of that corrected account to Turtle. Erasing the original will is unavailable because it would destroy the evidence. Quietly hiding the working sheet is insufficient: Turtle still needs the witness to retract the unsupported certainty.

The local feedback is the action itself: the false word disappears, the original is laid beside the corrected copy, and Turtle moves the suspect material aside to reconsider the actual wording.

### Corrected film and the reward

Repeat the question from the wrong film. This time Sydelle explains that she did not hear a completion. Turtle studies the original document and notices the relevant next heading. Later in the same room, after the others have left, she privately follows the directional names to an Eastman directory entry. Preserve her private deduction rather than making her reveal the secret to the entire group.

The paired close-up changes from an apparently certain added word to an acknowledged gap. Turtle's restored lead is the immediate success. An optional later film of her actual meeting with Eastman would require a separate office set and performances; it is not included in the one-room starter budget.

The student record distinguishes **observed**, **reported**, and **inferred** evidence. It does not turn a suspect board into proof of guilt.

### Two expansion concepts

- **Children are not permitted:** a fabricated participation rule excludes Turtle before she can contribute. Compare the new notice with authentic eligibility material and interview the person enforcing it. Restore her opportunity to take part rather than solving the game for her. The final authoring pass must establish a discoverable source for the false rule.
- **The clue becomes confetti:** a replacement notebook reaches Sydelle before the meeting. Trace the notebook through its handlers and recover the original. Paper scraps alone should not identify a thief; at least one necessary interview must establish the handoff. This concept needs its own earlier chapter boundary and cannot reuse the starter's final-solution spoilers.

## 3. The Phantom Tollbooth — Just One More Minute

### The original event and the invented change

**Suggested audience:** grades 5–6. **Reading boundary:** through Chapter 17; the tool's introduction in Chapter 16 is required. **Case ID proposal:** `tollbooth-stolen-staff`.

The Terrible Trivium occupies Milo, Tock, and the Humbug with absurd tasks. Milo uses the Mathemagician's pencil-like staff to work out that the work would take 837 years; recognizing the trap lets the companions leave. This is the restored turning point. See [Chapters 17–18](https://www.sparknotes.com/lit/tollbooth/section7/) and [Chapter 17 analysis](https://www.litcharts.com/lit/the-phantom-tollbooth/chapter-17-unwelcoming-committee).

In the altered case, the apparently helpful gentleman borrows Milo's staff for an invented “inspection,” conceals it in his open-latched work case, and returns an ordinary lookalike. Milo's attempt to calculate produces no useful result. The gentleman reassures him that the work will take only one more minute. This added theft prolongs the villain's original tactic without changing what the real staff does.

**Causal chain:** real staff concealed and imitation returned → Milo's attempted check fails → he relies on the gentleman's reassurance → the companions keep working → their rescue journey stalls.

The interface must not create an actual one-minute deadline. The villain's estimate is the lie under investigation.

### Opening film: approximately 35 seconds

1. An extraordinary mountain ledge: Milo transfers a grain of sand with tweezers while the camera pulls back to reveal the immense pile.
2. Tock carries a tiny drop between wells; the Humbug scratches the cliff with a needle. Their motions are precise and comically inadequate.
3. Milo tries the returned pencil. Nothing helpful happens. The faceless gentleman offers a soothing “Just one more minute.”
4. A short stylized passage of time shows the same tiny actions repeating while the gentleman remains perfectly composed. The path onward sits unused.
5. Hold beside Milo's work surface. Entry returns to the point before he accepts the failed calculation and settles back into work.

This is fantasy adventure with an unsettling figure, not horror. No jump scares, harm spectacle, or real-time task endurance are needed.

### The 3D set and its visual identity

A compact mountain clearing with enormous perceived depth: a narrow ledge, distant clouds, a warm sunset on stone, the two wells, the sand heap, and a small work case. The reachable set has five stations; the mountain beyond it is a finished vista rather than promised open-world terrain.

The look is cinematic storybook realism. Stone has scale and texture, water carries reflected light, Tock's clock has working depth and glass, and the gentleman's finely made suit sits over a deliberately smooth, featureless face. Whimsy comes from impossible relationships between believable objects. It does not require low-detail cartoon scenery or visual imitation of an adaptation.

| Position | Meaningful action |
| --- | --- |
| Milo's sand table | Interview Milo; perform one optional grain transfer; inspect the returned pencil |
| Wells | Perform a bounded three-drop experiment; compare the tiny change with the volume remaining |
| Cliff face | See the Humbug's negligible progress and inspect the work instructions |
| Gentleman's station | Interview him and challenge his estimate or claimed handling of the staff |
| Work case | Open the unlatched case after establishing a reason to inspect it; compare the two objects and recover Milo's staff |

Milo and the gentleman are the two required interviews. Tock and the Humbug need credible animated performances but not full dialogue trees for the starter. Their animation cost belongs in the art scope; two interviews do not mean only two characters exist.

### The mystery through interviews and tests

| Witness | Initial account | Evidence follow-up | What it establishes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Milo | **The gift:** the staff worked before they arrived; the gentleman briefly borrowed it, then handed one back. | Present the lookalike and ask about the handoff. Milo identifies the gesture and the work case the gentleman used. | Locates the relevant change in the tool's custody; he does not already know where the genuine object is now. |
| Gentleman | **The work:** insists it is nearly finished. **The staff:** initially implies that he only glanced at it. | Present Milo's account and the negligible progress from the well. He changes his story to claim he placed the original in safekeeping. The case becomes an evidence-backed inspection target. | Exposes the inconsistency between a quick look and retaining the original. His polished reassurance is not independent proof. |

The student can inspect the case's exterior at any time. Opening it is a contextual action once the ownership and custody issue is established. There is no unrelated key hunt, color lock, or dexterity challenge. The gentleman's attempt to draw attention back to the work is a performance, not a combat mechanic.

### Repair and proof

The main task is to recover the missing means of checking a claim. The optional drop experiment shows why the gentleman's estimate deserves scrutiny; the student is not asked to empty the wells or calculate the novel's exact number from an invented 3D volume.

The success rule requires:

1. A source reference connecting Milo's staff with the original recognition of the trap.
2. Milo's account of the loan and the gentleman's contradictory account of custody.
3. Inspection of the work case and recovery of the actual staff, whose identity Milo confirms.
4. Return of that staff to Milo and presentation of the work question so that he uses it.

The ordinary pencil and the authentic staff are different runtime objects. Switching their labels cannot change them. A claimed repair with the lookalike leaves Milo's calculation unresolved. Offering larger tools to finish the jobs fails the literary goal: the trap is accepting pointless work as the route forward.

The student hands Milo the real staff. Its immediate effect is specific: he can calculate, recognizes the scale of the work, and sets down the tweezers. The staff does not itself defeat the demon or transport everyone to the end of the novel. Milo retains the decision to reject the trap.

### Corrected film and the reward

Replay the same close-up of Milo trying to check the work. This time the real staff helps him establish the absurd time required. He looks up, exposes the gentleman's tactic, and leads his companions toward the path. The last shot leaves the abandoned tweezers in the foreground while the group disappears around the rock.

The next adventure is outside this case. The ending does not show the princesses rescued or combine the novel with a film adaptation's different defeat of the demon.

The contrast is unmistakable: in the wrong film they continue the futile motions; in the corrected film they stop and leave. The student's literary question is why stopping was progress.

### Two expansion concepts

- **The sunrise that never recovers:** the activity adds interference that prevents Chroma from resuming after Milo's chaotic attempt. Investigate the wake-up arrangements with Milo and Chroma and restore the handoff. Preserve Milo's original disastrous conducting rather than “fixing” it out of the novel. The original temporary chaos and lost week are discussed in this [interview with Norton Juster](https://www.salon.com/2001/03/12/juster/). Final authoring must make the added failure, rather than the original chaos, the explicit repair target.
- **The endless questions:** an added obstruction prevents the package of sounds from providing the laughter that breaks the Senses Taker's hold. Investigate the package and its handling, then release its contents at the right moment. This later case needs a separate chapter check and cast/set scope before scripting. It must not ask students to complete the villain's paperwork as the learning activity.

## Source and literary-fidelity review

The core turning points were checked against the linked sources for this design. No specific classroom edition has been selected or inspected. Before scripting production dialogue, the author must enter that edition, verify the chapter locators, select the supporting passages, and review each character's knowledge at the entry point. The expansion concepts are briefs, not source-complete case packages.

Use three distinct provenance labels in on-demand evidence details: **From the book**, **Added for this mystery**, and **Student inference**. Paraphrases must be identified as paraphrases. Activity dialogue must not masquerade as quotations. A character's new motive or handling of an invented object is part of the altered case, not a claim about the original author.

None of the mysteries should imply that every unpleasant original event is a mistake. Repair means returning this scene to its actual narrative role, including consequences that may remain difficult or ambiguous.

## Cinematic production and implementation boundary

The repository currently declares Angular 22 and Three.js 0.182.0 in `package.json`. This establishes an available interface framework and renderer, not a finished cinematic player or matching art assets. These designs depend on the reusable setup in the implementation specification; this turn did not audit or implement those runtime capabilities.

Create each master set and character family before making its final films. Render the wrong event, interview performances, and corrected event from those assets, then optimize the same assets for the interactive world. Compare matching frames at normal viewing size. Character faces, costumes, prop positions, camera perspective, material response, and ambient sound must remain coherent across the transition.

Do not call a generated concept picture a 3D set or a finished video. No cinematic media has been produced for this document. A browser-capable renderer alone does not guarantee the visual quality the user wants; character modeling, rigging, performance, lighting, animation, and optimization are distinct production tasks.

### Reusable capabilities to verify before implementation

| Requirement | Shared capability to verify or add |
| --- | --- |
| Travel among authored camera positions | Registered cinematic 3D activity renderer with accessible movement controls |
| Pick up, rotate, open, compare, and return objects | Configurable object actions and state variants |
| Present evidence to a witness | Scripted NPC topics and evidence-conditioned responses |
| Recognize a supported repair | Shared evidence/state conditions and commands, with declarative case-specific rules |
| Wrong film → world → corrected film | Media manifest, matching cameras, loading/error behavior, captions, and revision-aware outcome selection |

If a required ability is absent, report `TEMPLATE_CAPABILITY_GAP` for that reusable ability. Do not add book-name checks to generic services, invent a connected AI tutor, or produce three bespoke application pages. The book names belong in content and configuration.

### Candidate comparison and build order

| Consideration | Frindle | The Westing Game | The Phantom Tollbooth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starter set | One living room | One apartment investigation room | One mountain clearing with distant vista |
| Required interview characters | Nick and Alice | Sydelle and Turtle | Milo and the gentleman |
| Extra performance scope | Parents in the original interview | Limited gathering/aftermath presence | Tock and Humbug performing their tasks |
| Reading demand | Intent and accurate representation | Inference, exact wording, source reliability | Allegory, persuasion, means versus purpose |
| Visual payoff | Embarrassment turns into an accurate report | A false certainty becomes a productive clue | A seemingly endless trap is abandoned |
| Main production risk | Convincing child/adult faces and believable recording props | Readable document interaction and clear explanation of a complex ending | Creature animation and a convincing fantasy vista |

**Recommended first playable trial: Frindle.** Its cause is easy to see, its room is compact, and the two versions of the report make success concrete. **Recommended visual showcase: The Phantom Tollbooth.** **Recommended deeper reading investigation: The Westing Game.** These are design recommendations; no pilot has been registered or built.

A single case does not fulfill the earlier four-week/eight-session project plan. Each book's expansions need further source review and distinct investigation designs before they can fill later sessions. Teachers may also use one starter as a standalone case after the specified reading.

## Acceptance checklist for a later build

- A student can identify the wrong event from the film without an explanatory essay.
- The transition enters real 3D at a matching shot and makes its earlier time clear.
- Two required witnesses supply different necessary facts; each respects its knowledge boundary.
- The added cause is discoverable before the repair. Neither a confession from nowhere nor a colored “correct” object is sufficient.
- Every permitted successful path has source evidence and a real installed change. Reordering valid discovery steps remains possible.
- At least one plausible wrong intervention receives meaningful local feedback and can be revised.
- The corrected film shows the exact restored milestone, preserves the novel's original surprises, and stays inside the reading boundary.
- Captions, transcripts, reduced motion, keyboard, touch, non-drag actions, and readable evidence support the full case.
- Reload, direct entry, replay, retry, and revising a prior success have verified behavior.
- Visual and runtime checks are performed on the target student devices using actual media and finished assets.
